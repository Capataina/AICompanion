# World observation — one shared account of the world

This folder rebuilds facts from Terraria once per brain tick. It observes; it never decides, chooses a position, plans a route, or writes movement. `CoordinateBrainTick` passes its aggregate to reflex assessment and behaviour selection, so a new fact belongs here when more than one downstream system could otherwise derive it differently.

```
Observation/
├─ CLAUDE.md                 this guide
├─ Senses.cs                 aggregate and update order
├─ ObserveCompanion.cs       breath, liquid, fire, life and recent damage; derives self danger
├─ ObservePlayer.cs          position, travel intent, fighting and sight
├─ InferPlayerActivity.cs    bounded displacement/work evidence and travel confidence
├─ ObservePlayerWork.cs      player tool hits and terrain-change notification
├─ ObserveThreats.cs         hostile records, reachability, danger and safety horizon
├─ ObserveEncounterContext.cs whether the world is about one boss or event, from native facts or inferred from sustained pressure
├─ EstimateEffectiveDamage.cs what one hit takes off the player or the companion after defence, by the game's arithmetic
├─ ObserveHostileAttackSources.cs native hostile projectile ownership and generation reset
├─ ObserveProjectiles.cs     hostile projectile predictions for reflexes
├─ PredictObservedMotion.cs  shared terrain-constrained enemy forecast used by aiming and reflexes
├─ CalculateRegroupUrgency.cs pure return-pressure calculation from distance and travel cost
├─ ObserveLoot.cs            collectible drops that fit a player stack or bag
├─ ObserveLight.cs           brightness per open-air tile on a lattice, read only where the engine computed it, with every carried light discounted
├─ ReadTransientLights.cs    the engine's own per-frame light list: everything that is in the world only while something carries it
├─ ObserveReach.cs           where the body can walk to and come home from, as a tri-state every consumer reads
├─ ThreatRecord.cs           a hostile and its predicted hitbox
└─ LineOfSight.cs            the named game line-of-sight query
```

`Senses.Update` runs player, threats, projectiles, loot, light and self observation before selection reads any value; the reach sense is the one member it does not drive, for the reason given below. Player travel inference retains bounded two-axis displacement and local-work evidence. Net displacement relative to distance travelled distinguishes a journey from repeated local movement; sample support and recent work temper confidence. Both climbing and descent remain visible. Separate player and self danger are intentional: leaving a threatened player and walking into danger are different risks. Threats also derive a safety horizon, which selection uses to discount a behaviour that would keep the companion away too long.

A threat's consequence is what its hit costs the body it would land on, not the attacker's raw number. `EstimateEffectiveDamage` reproduces the game's damage after defence — the player's defence at the effectiveness the game assigns by difficulty plus endurance, the companion's defence at the fixed NPC half — by calling the native modifier arithmetic directly, without running loader hit hooks, because a mod may spend a shield charge or play a sound inside a hook that exists for real hits. Each threat then weighs that effective hit against a quarter of the life the victim has left: armour lowers urgency, a wounded body raises it, and the player and the companion differ exactly where the game makes the same hit cost them differently (the companion copies the player's defence but keeps the NPC effectiveness). At full health with no defence the weight equals the older raw-damage share, so the danger thresholds keep the meaning they were tuned with for an unhurt, unarmoured body. Downstream of that weight sit player and companion danger, protection urgency, the safety horizon, guard pressure, hunt candidate ordering and the arsenal's candidate order and prevented-harm danger; the arsenal's own prevented-harm amount still reads raw expected damage, so its algorithm is unchanged while its inputs are not. Modded resistances applied through hit hooks are not reflected, and the estimate is the median hit because contact damage variation is left out.

Whether the world is currently about one thing — a boss fight or a world event — is an observation, `Senses.Encounter`, updated right after threats because it reads them. It answers from the state of the world and never from a monster's identity. Recognised sources are native facts: an observed hostile carrying the game's boss flag at any depth, the Old One's Army at any depth, and a blood moon, solar eclipse, pumpkin or frost moon or active invasion only while the player is at or above the surface line, which is where the game runs them. A player inside a lunar pillar's zone is recognised too, because the spawner raises its invasion flag there and the pillars themselves carry no boss flag, deal no damage and weigh no spawn slots, so nothing else would name them. Slime rain is deliberately not an encounter. An invasion counts only where the game would send its enemies to this player, which is the spawner's own gate rather than the invasion flag: arrived (no delay left), enemies still to send, the player above one screen below the surface line, and within the invasion's spawning band. An event nothing names — a modded invasion that sets no vanilla flag — cannot be recognised, so the fallback is observed pressure measured against the game's own spawn accounting. The game's spawner adds enemies near a player only while the spawn-slot weight of NPCs around that player is below a cap it recomputes every tick from depth, biome, time, events, buffs and every mod's spawn-rate hook, this mod's doubling included. The spawner admits another enemy only while that weight is under the cap in force at that moment, so ordinary spawning can never lift the weight above the highest cap applied since the oldest enemy still counted arrived, the admission ceiling, and pressure is the reachable hostiles weighing more than that ceiling: enemies arriving from somewhere ordinary spawning would not have allowed. The ceiling is measured over time rather than read from the tick's cap because the cap follows the player's own state while a crowd stays put: a calming potion, a peace candle, town NPCs moving in, a change of depth, or this mod's own doubling lapsing while the companion is downed all lower it under an unchanged crowd. Arrivals are kept per hostile by slot, type and spawn generation and survive a step out of range, and the caps are kept as a run in which a later cap at least as high replaces the earlier ones, so the ceiling costs a handful of entries however long a crowd stays. The ceiling can only overstate what spawning admitted, so its error is a missed inference rather than a false one: enemies arriving under a lowered cap but still under the old ceiling are not inferred. The weight mirrors the game's count exactly — each NPC's slots, only inside the game's active range of the player, and nothing for an NPC that does not despawn to inactivity, which is how the game counts a worm as its head alone — restricted to hostiles able to reach either actor, so enemies walled off from both do not count. The cap is the game's private spawn-cap field, bound by reflection and read as the spawner left it; the spawner runs before the NPC update the companion's AI runs in, so it is this tick's value except on ticks the spawner skipped (straight after any despawn or death, with the player dead or beside the Moon Lord), which keep the last computed one. Pressure ramps in over a window, ends the first tick the weight is back at or under the ceiling, and is always marked unrecognised so a reader can tell an inferred encounter from a known one. Its blind spot follows from the design: a modded event that raises the cap and spawns through the vanilla spawner never exceeds the cap it raised, so only enemies added outside ordinary spawning (a mod calling NPC creation directly) are inferred. The observation only reports; the shared evaluator is the one place it changes a value, and the wider leash and survival-first conduct the README describes for encounters are not built on it yet. The ramp length lives in BehaviourWeights.

The first fallback was a count of observed hostile records able to reach an actor, at a fixed threshold, and it failed as a property rather than a tuning: a count of NPC records measures threat-list size, not whether the world has changed. Threat observation has no distance filter and keeps every segment of a multi-segment body, so one Giant Worm underground was eight records, an ordinary cave under the doubled spawn cap passed any small threshold, and both stopped every optional activity; raising the threshold would only have moved the guess until the next spawn-rate change broke it. The second rule compared the weight with the cap in force on the tick and failed as a property too: the cap is a function of the player's current state, so every choice a player makes to thin spawns lowered it under a crowd that had not moved and read as enemies arriving. Both failures compared a crowd against a number that was not the baseline the rule assumed; the admission ceiling is the one the spawner actually enforces.

The intent history advances once per observed engine tick. Missing intervals, death and large position corrections clear its evidence rather than inventing a traversed path. Recent pick/axe contacts and active tile/wall placement are local-work evidence, not a command to remain still or proof that placement succeeded. A short reversal weakens an existing journey before sustained backtracking replaces it; a pause ages motion out. The confidence-weighted vector feeds reunion's meeting places, position preference and return pressure, while raw velocity remains available for immediate physical questions. Prediction is bounded to the observation horizon and is not a known destination or a route the companion can perform. Tunables live in BehaviourWeights.

`PlayerSense.Interference` is the one courtesy fact: the tiles the player is asking the companion to vacate, derived from what the player is already doing rather than from the cursor alone. A held solid, non-platform block or a wall aimed within the player's placement range at a tile the companion's body covers yields that tile; a grounded player walking at travel speed under a roof directly over a standing body, with the companion nearby on that floor, yields the passage ahead of the player while the roof continues, bounded. The passage is measured from the player, never from the companion, because anchoring it on the companion let every step the companion took move the footprint's edge, and the spot just past that edge was still in the player's way. A torch is deliberately not placement evidence here even though torch placement is local-work evidence for travel intent above: a torch does not close the space a body stands in. The footprint is held for a bounded window after it was last seen and carries a revision that moves when a different footprint appears. It is evidence, not a command, and whether a player would physically be blocked by the companion's body is not established: the fixtures move the player by position.

A recent-velocity smoother can report confident travel after the player repeatedly crosses the same small area. The local-motion regression exercises that ambiguity through PlayerSense itself; pure sequence tests additionally cover work, reversal, pause, vertical travel, duplicate ticks and observation gaps. These do not establish recognition of every player intention. Turning the interpretation into a place to meet belongs to position selection's meeting places, whose activity pairs end the player on one tile after different recent activity.

Player tool hits are evidence, not animation guessed as intent. `ObservePlayerWork` records real axe and pick hits for work behaviours and tells shared movement whenever a relevant tile changes, invalidating cached movement facts. Doors report their own changes because the game’s door helper bypasses those tile hooks.

## Light is a field, and darkness is a question you ask of a place

`ObserveLight.cs` stores a brightness per open-air tile on a coarse lattice through a window around the companion, and every consumer asks it about somewhere rather than reading one number. A mean over the whole window stood here before and could not answer the question anyone asks: averaged across a lit chamber and the dark wings beside it, the mean is wrong about both, so the companion put a torch up in a lit room and left a cave beside it dark. Three queries replace it. `DarkAirNear` is the share of measured air under the dark level within a radius, which the torch decision reads twice — around the body and at the player's predicted feet, so a torch is up before the companion reaches the hole rather than after. `NearestDarkRegion` and `DarkRegionsNearest` cluster adjacent dark samples into areas and rank them by the nearest dark air in each, which is what lighting walks to. `MeasuredAround` answers the same darkness question about one site by sampling the engine directly at a finer stride, because a lattice cannot resolve anything narrower than its own step and a lit patch smaller than the stride can sit entirely between samples.

Packed tiles are not darkness, and `IsOpenAir` is the one place that ruling lives, so every query inherits it. Averaging solid tiles in made a block of stone read as a dark room and hid the real air pockets.

**A zero is not darkness, because the engine answers zero for a tile it has not computed.** The field reads brightness only inside the active engine's own computed bounds, snapshotted once per refresh, so a tile outside is absent rather than dark and an engine that has never presented a frame measures nothing at all. `MeasuredSamples` and `ReadTick` say so out loud, and a query over unread air answers `Unmeasured`, which holds a decision rather than making one. The native colour engine's GetColor checks its presented `_activeProcessedArea`; ProcessScan fills a different working map, and Present swaps the completed map and its area together after blur. Legacy modes instead index `_states` relative to the requested rectangle and OffScreenTiles, with camera-sized bounds, assigned before the processed values are copied. So neither the current camera rectangle nor an allocated buffer proves a completed observation. Reproduce with `sh Tools/decompile.sh Terraria.Lighting`, `… Terraria.Graphics.Light.LightingEngine` and `… Terraria.Graphics.Light.LegacyLighting`. Only the colour-mode bounds are exercised headlessly (`Tools/EngineReplay/Assistance/VerifyUsefulAssistance.cs` writes the engine's own light map and area); the legacy bounds rest on the decompiled source, because the headless legacy engine has no camera. The window is screen-sized and centred on the companion but never clipped to where the screen is, because the coverage test is that question asked exactly and clipping only discards readings the engine has; it is also never smaller than a neighbourhood around the body, since with no screen at all a screen-derived window collapses to one column and reads as a world where nothing has been measured.

**Every light somebody is carrying is discounted, and the sense never learns which kind it is.** The engine already separates the two kinds and it separates them exactly where this needs them: world light — placed torches, lava, glowing tiles, sunlight — comes from the tile scanner, and everything transient arrives through `Lighting.AddLight`, which the colour engine collects into a private per-frame list. So the rule is "read the list", not "know every source": a player's held torch, a light pet, a glowstick in flight, a mining helmet and a lantern from a mod nobody here has heard of are in it by construction, and so is the companion's own torch, which stopped being a special case. `ReadTransientLights.cs` owns that read. Without it the companion follows a player carrying a torch, reads everything near him as lit, places nothing, and the passage is dark the moment he walks on — the opposite of lighting a mine as you go.

**Which batch to read is settled by the engine's own cycle rather than estimated.** `LightingEngine.ProcessArea` is a four-state machine — minimap, metrics, scan, blur — advancing one state per call, so the list accumulates across up to four frames and is consumed only on the blur state: `ProcessBlur` calls `ApplyPerFrameLights`, which merges each entry and clears the list, and `Present` then swaps the working map into the active one every reader sees. The batch the last blur cleared is therefore not an approximation of the transients in the map being read — it is exactly them. The sense observes once per brain tick, sees the list grow and drop to nothing, and keeps the snapshot taken immediately before a drop; the drop is how the clear is detected without reaching into a private method. Before any drop has been seen — the opening frames of a world, and every headless fixture, where nothing drives `ProcessArea` — the gathered view stands in, and is correct rather than merely available, because with no blur having run nothing has been consumed. A MonoMod hook on `AddLight` was the alternative and lost twice over: the field is plainly readable at our own tick so the hook buys no access, and it would record calls made after the blur as though they were in the map. Sources are merged by tile as they are read, which is both what the engine does and what stops a light re-added every frame from becoming one entry per frame.

**A carried light is discounted by the engine's own combination rule, and that rule is maximum, not addition — established from the decompile rather than assumed.** `LightMap.BlurLine` keeps a running value per channel and, for each tile along the line, takes `if (zero.X < _colors[i].X) zero.X = _colors[i].X;` and otherwise writes the running value back into the tile, multiplying it by `LightDecayThroughAir` (0.91) or `LightDecayThroughSolid` (0.56) per step and abandoning the channel below 0.0185. There is no addition anywhere in that loop. `LightingEngine.ApplyPerFrameLights` merges each transient in with `Vector3.Max` before that blur runs. So a light never adds to a tile: it either loses to what was there or replaces it.

That settles the arithmetic, and it is the branch where subtraction does not work. Where the observed value exceeds what the transient could have produced, the excess is not "most of" the world's light — the observed value *is* the world's light, exactly, and is kept whole. Where it does not, the world's light is at most the observed value and nothing narrows it further, which is the bound case the two consumers read in opposite directions through `carriedCountsAsDark`. Had the engine summed, subtraction would be exact and the bound case would never fire; it does not sum. Subtracting a modelled torch from a maximum is therefore meaningless arithmetic, and it produces a false dark blob at the hand — a companion that would walk to its own feet to light them. A sample is instead kept whole when it is brighter than the torch could possibly make it, since that brightness is the world's own; and dropped as unmeasured when the torch could account for it and the torch there is above the dark level, because then every ambient from pitch black up to the torch looks identical and the reading answers nothing. Where the torch's own reach is below the dark level the sample is kept, because the place is dark and reads dark either way. The torch model decays 0.91 per tile through air, Manhattan because the blur runs a horizontal then a vertical pass, and stops below 0.0185 where the engine's propagation stops; it is deliberately an upper bound, since it decays only through air where the engine decays through solid at 0.56, so it hides a few samples the torch never reached rather than crediting the room with the companion's own light.

**The discount depends on a flag that must be true when it is read, and being one call too early made it permanently false.** The sense asks whether the companion's torch was out, and the only true answer is the previous tick's, because the light map in front of it was written by the previous tick's `AddLight`. The NPC tick used to clear that state at its top, ahead of the brain, so the sense read "no torch" on every live tick and the whole discount above was dead code in the game while passing every fixture, because the fixtures set the flag themselves. `CompanionNPC.AI` therefore recomputes the torch after the brain and hides it only on the paths that never reach that recomputation. The general shape: a fact one component publishes for another to read has an order, and a fixture that arranges the fact instead of letting the producer publish it cannot see the order at all.

**A sample the companion's own torch accounts for means opposite things to the two consumers, and both readings are right.** Deciding whether to *hold* a torch, it is unknown and skipped: the world's own light there lies somewhere between nothing and the torch's contribution, and reading either end lets the torch decide itself. Deciding whether to *place* one, it is evidence, because the bound runs the useful way — the world's light there is at most what the companion is carrying, and the companion is about to walk off with it. `MeasuredAround` takes `carriedCountsAsDark` for exactly this, and the placement consumer is the one that sets it. Reading it as room light in both places is a deadlock rather than caution: the torch comes out because the cavern is dark, every site then reads lit precisely because the torch is out, and nothing is ever placed.

The held torch, its light and map reveal still follow the interaction layer's `Shown` answer; observation does not decide whether the hand is free. Light read from the field is never permission to spend a supplied torch: placement asks its own question about the site.

## Reach is the other sense, and its middle answer is the point

`ObserveReach.cs` owns the two floods from the companion's feet that position selection used to keep to itself: one refusing edges with no way back, which is the region meaning everywhere the body can go and come home from, and a raw one advanced only to test the single exception of a player standing somewhere the body can only drop into. Being stuck is not having no way back to the take-off; it is having no way to the player, so a place he is standing in is by definition not somewhere the companion strands itself.

`Reachable` answers Reachable, NotYet or Unreachable, and the middle value is why it is a sense rather than a boolean. A tile missing from a flood that has not finished is unknown, not absent, so an activity refusing NotYet is declining to start on an unanswered search rather than asserting the place does not exist — the ruling that optional work does not start on an unanswered search, expressible in the type instead of reconstructed by each caller.

**Every activity and every interaction now reads this and none of them searches, and that is enforced rather than agreed.** `Tools/check-navigation-boundary.sh` refuses any route search named under `Activities` or `Interactions`; those folders may hold `Reachability.Reach`, which is the verdict they pass around, and may not call `WalkerReach`, `RoundTrip`, `AStar.Find` or their kin. The rule is worth the enforcement because breaking it is invisible at the call site: a bounded search per candidate answers Unknown on its expansion budget, a caller cannot remember an Unknown without writing a place off on evidence that does not exist, and a caller that rations itself therefore re-asks the same nearest candidates for ever. The 2026-09-14 capture spent 79% of its lighting offers saying the question could not be finished, with a settled flood beside 7,003 of those rows holding the answer.

**A consumer that asks during a hold gets a region nobody is growing.** The `Hold` branch of the positioner's resolve returns before it reaches `Refresh`, so a stretch of ticks where whatever won asks for a hold ages the region without advancing it. Resolve is not the only way in, though, and the difference matters: `ChooseUsefulPosition.IsReturnable` refreshes before it answers, so any consumer asking that *query* grows the flood as a side effect of asking. `TheReachFloodSettles` in SessionReport is the instrument for it, and on the 2026-09-14 capture it reports one unbroken stretch of 6,478 rows, from tick 1, where the flood never finished. That is the thing to check first when optional work is not starting and every offer reads honestly.

**The hold gap is one edge of a circle, and what opens it is a query rather than a decision.** With no region at all — spawn, and every terrain revision — work reads NotYet and offers zero, so keeping company wins, and a resting companion asks for a hold, which does not refresh. Nothing the brain *decides* leaves that state. What does leave it is `KeepCompany.SafeStrollGoal` asking `IsReturnable` of each candidate stroll tile: that query refreshes before answering, so on a null region the first pick rejects every candidate — because the region is null — and grows the region while doing so, and a later pick finds goals. The first flood of a companion's life therefore arrives as a side effect of a test that failed.

That is worth stating plainly because it is not a design anyone would choose, and it is one tidy-up away from being lost: moving the refresh out of the query and into the resolve, or short-circuiting a candidate scan on an empty region, would each close the circle while reading as a cleanup. `Age` never clears the region, only stales it, so this bites at a null region and not at an old one. `VerifyOreWork.AColdFloodDoesNotLeaveTheBrainResting` is the standing check, and it detects the closed case rather than being assumed to: neutering `Refresh` on a null region freezes the companion ten pixels from spawn with `keep-company` holding every tick. Removing the refresh from the positioner's `Exact` branch alone does *not* redden it, which is how the opener was identified rather than guessed.

**A verdict of absent must rest on the exhaustion of the set it was looked up in, and there are two sets here.** `Complete` is the two-way flood's, which is the flood `Reachable` answers from; `ScoredComplete` is the scored region's, which on a player-only-one-way tick is the raw region instead. One flag serving both let a tile the two-way flood had not yet reached be graded Unreachable because the raw flood had finished — the same not-yet-is-not-no error the middle answer exists to prevent, arriving through the completeness rather than through the membership. It bites harder than it reads, because a consumer that remembers a proven No keeps it for the whole no-return wait rather than re-asking on the next rescore, so the mistake does not correct itself.

**The flood's unit of time is the rescore, not the game tick.** `Age` is called once per resolve and `Refresh` refloods when that count passes, because the flood is bounded per advance and grows across successive resolves: a caller resolving repeatedly inside one tick is asking the region to keep expanding, and a cadence keyed to the tick advances it once and never finishes. Readers never age it, so a consumer asking a question during a hold cannot expire the region out from under the scoring it is about to be compared against. A terrain revision forces a reflood inside the sense, so a consumer that never goes through a resolve cannot serve a region from before the dig.

The sense is refreshed by position selection's resolve rather than by `Senses.Update`, and that is deliberate: the lava and one-way rules are set per request by the brain tick, so a flood run at the top of the tick would answer every consumer under the previous tick's rule. Consumers reading between resolves get the region the last resolve left, a rescore old at worst.

LootSense owns the world-slot membership predicate used by prepared-item activation and collection. An object reference identifies the selected drop, but its active flag can remain true after the world table replaces it. Availability therefore checks the actual table entry as well as quantity and activity. This consumes current identity evidence without rediscovering a different target or rescoring its reward.

## Traps

- A harmful hostile and an attackable target are different observations. Threat collection keeps harmful NPCs even when Terraria declines `CanBeChasedBy`; the arsenal applies that targetability filter when it considers a shot. Player death clears player protection pressure, but does not erase danger to the companion or its retreat anchor.
- Motion continuation carries measured one-tick forecast error and a decaying confidence. Low confidence shortens protection time conservatively; it does not invent type-specific future AI.

Reachability is destination-specific: `CanReachPlayer` gates player urgency and the safety horizon, while `CanReachCompanion` gates personal urgency and retreat eligibility. The two results use the same bounded searches and staggered refresh, with a shared answer only when their destination tiles coincide. A cached negative becomes unknown/potential danger when the enemy, destination, movement class or terrain changes; that invalidation does not add unbudgeted searches. Neither endpoint's reachability determines whether an enemy occupies a proposed future position or route.

Enemy predictions share one observed-motion history across aiming and reflexes. Consecutive observations supply acceleration; collision impulses and abrupt jumps are not repeated as acceleration. The forecast snapshots each NPC's current `gravity`, `maxFallSpeed`, liquid flags and movement-speed fields, then uses native tile and slope collision with the same wet slowdown before restoring the game's shared collision scratch state. Forecasts are cached per observed entity state and tick, and cleared on spawn, death and world closure. They predict current motion over a bounded horizon; they cannot know an arbitrary modded enemy's next scripted decision or reproduce a future NPC-specific step/slope policy. Hostile projectile velocity is converted from sub-update units to game-tick units before reflex projection.

The predictor primes itself from an observation, then measures its next observed continuation against the forecast. Confidence is accompanied by its sample count; zero samples is unmeasured, never proof of stable enemy motion. Native projectile ownership is attributed at spawn with generation-aware identity, so a reused projectile slot cannot inherit an earlier hostile's attack source.

- Never calculate a world fact inside a behaviour: selection runs every behaviour every tick and duplicated readings drift.
- `PlayerDanger` and `SelfDanger` answer different questions. A formula of `1 - danger` must name whose danger it consumes.
- The player’s recorded trail can contain a double-jump route the companion cannot make; it is evidence for a scenario, not proof the planner is defective.
