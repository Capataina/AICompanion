# Steering — a route, and the momentum that follows it

The navigator owns one route and one search at a time and turns them into a velocity every tick; the steering law is the pure function under it. Nothing here writes the body: the answer is a `Controls`, the velocity the motor should accelerate toward, and the motor is what applies it.

```

├─ CLAUDE.md            this guide
├─ OrbState.cs          the body at one tick: centre, velocity, the liquid it touches, whether it is pinned
├─ Controls.cs          what the brain asks for one tick: the velocity to move toward, and whether it may burst there
├─ OrbPace.cs           the speed cap, turn authority and speed change the motor applies, and the one law that applies them
├─ Route.cs             the points from the body to the goal, the segment the body is on, validity, and line-of-sight smoothing
├─ SteerAlongRoute.cs   the steering law: a lookahead that grows with speed, an eased arrival, a cap through the next bend
├─ HoverAroundSpot.cs   what the body does wherever it has been asked to be: a slow wander around the spot, kept off walls
├─ EvadeWhileMoving.cs  safety on top of the job: the tick's controls bent away from a predicted hit, only when they would meet one
├─ Navigator.cs         plans, re-plans, steers, hovers on arrival or while a goal is out of reach, and scores how each attempt ended
└─ BehaviourCensus.cs   the whole run's accounting: places asked for, reached and abandoned, plans made and found nothing
```

## The law

The body is steered at a point ahead of its projection on the route, which is what makes it lean into a turn and cut its inside rather than track every waypoint, and a body that has drifted off the route is steered at the lookahead from its projection, which pulls it back onto the route rather than back to where it left. The lookahead is a number of ticks of the body's own speed, clamped between a floor and a ceiling, so a fast body looks further down the route and curves into a bend early while a slow one does not cut a corner it is barely moving through. The speed asked for is the cap, lowered toward the goal so the body eases in rather than overshoots — the speed from which only a share of the speed change would bring it to rest over what is left, which is what makes the slowdown gradual rather than a late stop — and lowered into a bend in proportion to how sharply the route turns at the next waypoint within braking distance.

**The motor's law has two caps, one on each axis of the heading, and `OrbPace.Step` is the only place it is written.** The change in velocity across the heading is capped by the turn authority, so the turning radius at speed is the square of the speed over it; the change along the heading is capped by the speed change, which is several times smaller, so the body gathers speed and sheds it over most of a second while still turning tightly. Below a walking pace there is no heading to split along, and the whole change takes the speed-change cap. A dodge sets `Controls.Burst` and may spend the turn authority along its heading as well, which is the one move meant to look sudden. A body that cannot turn inside a corridor's width scrapes the far wall whatever the route says; the corridor fixture's minimum-clearance row is where that showed, the turn authority is what fixed it, and splitting the speed change off it is what stopped that fix from snapping every departure to full speed in twelve ticks. NavReplay's arrival row carries the ramp as a measure and a declared band, and every headless tool integrates this same function rather than a copy.

## A route is smoothed without giving back what the search paid for

Smoothing skips waypoints while the straight segment past them keeps the body clear of walls, from the front, using the contact's own swept test. It also refuses a skip where the chord, at the point standing in for a skipped waypoint, has less clearance than that waypoint had by more than the half tile the corner lattice cannot resolve, because the search paid for the middle of a corridor through its edge cost and a chord from one wall-hugging end of a corridor to the other is clear of walls while spending its whole length nearer them. The first smoother did exactly that and undid the search, and the corridor fixture's smoothed row is what refuses it now.

## Arrival, replanning, and what the navigator reports

Arrival is a radius around the goal, and staying arrived is a wider one. Inside the arrival radius the navigator drops its route and hovers around the goal; it keeps reading arrived until the body leaves the settle radius, which is the arrival radius plus the hover's own reach plus a margin, so a drifting body does not flicker between arrived and travelling. The settle radius, not the arrival radius, is what every region the positioner admits a destination against reserves, so a spot on a region's boundary cannot be hovered around outside it. `Tools/NavReplay`'s arrival row drives that tick by tick, holds it for a hundred and twenty ticks on one search, and requires the body never to sit still for the session reader's still threshold. A goal that drifts a little re-aims the last segment; one that moves materially cancels the attempt and plans afresh. A goal whose search has not finished is flown toward along a partial route to the nearest place that search has reached, and so is a goal whose search stopped at its node limit; a goal the search has proven unreachable is hovered around a wait anchor taken where the body was when it stopped being able to go, and is published as proven unreachable, which the brain bans the spot on, rather than flown toward, because another place is the positioner's answer. Neither is answered with nothing: nothing is a body standing still. **A finished search without a route is not run again for the same goal** until the terrain it read changes, the body leaves what a proven absence explored, or a node-limited search's partial route has brought the body materially nearer; the first build ran the same proof on every tick for as long as the goal was held. **A kept conclusion answers the goal it was reached about, not the goal the navigator held a tick ago.** Each one records its own goal and is forgotten once the goal is further than the replan distance from that record, because the drift branch re-aims the held goal a little every tick: compared tick to tick a smoothly moving goal never counts as moved, and a sentinel's review of the first build found a settled-short answer and a proven absence each surviving six hundred pixels of drift, the body parked 583 px from a goal a fresh navigator reached. **Only a finished search's own choice settles the body short.** A route that ends at the free corner beside a goal the body cannot slide into is an answer about that goal; a partial route lent by a running or node-limited search is not, and the difference is carried as a flag set where the search finishes, never inferred from what else happens to be kept — inferring it from a missing node-limit answer meant a tile edit anywhere in a large search parked the body short for good. A no-progress strike does not clear either conclusion, because a strike while hovering at a kept answer is that answer being obeyed. `Tools/NavReplay/VerifyNavigatorConclusions.cs` holds each of these, and the interrupt scene in it holds the one that is easiest to lose: a node-limit answer proves nothing about its goal, so it dies with the attempt that asked, or a resumed attempt from the same place is never earned and lends no partial route. **Every hover anchor dies with the request that made it and is never somewhere the body cannot fly straight to.** The wait anchor is dropped by a new goal, an interrupt and a clear, and replaced by the body's own centre whenever the swept line to it is blocked; the first build kept it across an interrupted wait, and the next unreachable goal pulled the body 230 px back toward the old place and held it at zero against the wall between. `Tools/NavReplay`'s anchors row carries a body across a wall mid-wait to hold that.

## Hovering is a pursuit of a wandering target, and both halves of that were learned by measuring

`HoverAroundSpot` is Reynolds' wander: a target circles the spot along a flattened ellipse, and the rate it circles at is itself a small random walk with a floor, so the path keeps turning and never parks, and it reverses direction now and then so it is not an orbit. Its seed is per instance, so a headless run is reproducible. Three things about it are properties rather than tuning, and each was found by a trace rather than by reading.

- **The body pursues the target and does not seek it.** It asks for the target's own motion plus a correction toward it, capped at a drift's speed around a spot. A seek asks for a speed proportional to the gap, and a body a few pixels from its target slowed to a tenth of a pixel a tick every time the target doubled back — a body standing still with extra steps.
- **A target mirrored off a wall reverses its direction of circling with it.** Reflecting a circular path reverses its angular velocity; mirroring only the offset bounced the target straight back into the ceiling it had just been reflected off. When no mirror is clear the target falls back to the opposite point, then half the offset, then the spot itself with the circling reversed.
- **The request never presses the body into a wall it would meet; the part pointing into the wall is mirrored, not removed.** The target is clear of walls from the spot, not from the body, and a body lagging round the ellipse meets corners the target's path never touched. The contact already kills the into-wall part of a velocity and keeps the slide, but a request pointing almost straight at a step's corner leaves no slide, and on a staircase of blocks the body sat under the still threshold for fourteen ticks asking into the same step. The request is resolved against the contact first and mirrored off the net push-out — the net push rather than one tile's normal, because an inner corner is two tiles and a mirror off either face sends the body into the other. `VerifyCompanyLocalMotion`'s block-staircase row found it and holds it; its longest still run went from fourteen ticks to four.

The anchor is taken once, where the hover began, and not from the body each tick, because an anchor that moves with the body turns the drift into a slow wander away from where it arrived.

**Keeping the player company is the same wander across a moving box, and the four ways it differs are reasons, not values.** `HoverAroundSpot.Across` walks the target over the player's intent region, handed to it as a centre, a half-size and a lead rather than as the region itself, because the movement core names none of the senses it is fed by; `CoordinateMovement.Accompany` releases any held route first, since inside the region there is nowhere to go. The target lives in the box's own frame, so the region's travel reaches the pursuit through the target's own motion and nothing adds the region's velocity a second time. The heading's turning rate is far slower than the spot hover's and walks through zero with no floor, because a floor forces curvature: with one the heading closed loops and an idle companion never reached the far third of the box. The pursuit is capped at the body's top speed rather than a drift's, because a body capped at a drift inside a box moving at a running player's pace falls out of the back of it and is sent home as outside. And while the box leads, its rear is closed to the target, which keeps a travelling player's companion level with him or ahead rather than trailing as a policy; the box's own top is the ceiling, because the region sits mostly above the player by the owner's ruling and a second ceiling under its top would cut that part away. A refused target — one the circle cannot sweep to from the last target, or one inside the player's interference footprint — turns the walk round; where every turn is refused too, the target jumps outward along the axes to the nearest place that is allowed and swept clear from the body. That jump is what moves a body standing on the tile the player is building on or in the one-body-tall passage he is walking down, because a step is a pixel and a half and a refused tile is sixteen, so every turned step from inside the footprint is refused as well. The reach flood refuses nothing here: the swept test already keeps the walk in free space connected to the body, and a place the flood holds no corner for can still be one the circle sweeps straight to.

## Evading is a bend, and it runs only when the job's own flight would be hit

`EvadeWhileMoving` flies the job forward through the motor's law and the contact for a short horizon against the reflex's predicted-collision predicate and against any liquid that hurts the body. If that flight stays clear it returns the job's controls untouched, which is almost every tick. If not, it scores a ring of headings at full speed plus the job's own heading and a stop, as context steering does: danger is how soon a heading is hit, every heading within a small tolerance of the least dangerous is eligible, and among those the one with the most interest wins — the job's heading carries interest, a stop carries negative interest, so the body only stops where stopping is safer than every heading that keeps it going by more than the danger tolerance.

**The keep test flies the job's own steering, not the tick's velocity held in a straight line.** The first tick of the flight applies the controls the job produced; every later tick asks a forecast what the job would ask from where the simulation has put the body. `CoordinateMovement` builds the forecast from whichever request produced the tick and the branch the navigator reports it steered by (`Navigator.LastSteering`): a route is steered by `SteerAlongRoute` with the forecast's own copy of the segment index, a direct line is the same eased ask toward the goal, a hover is `HoverAroundSpot.Pursue` toward the target it last chose, held still for the horizon because the hover's speed keeps that target's path short, and keeping the player company is `HoverAroundSpot.PursueAcross` toward its walking target carried on by the target's last motion each simulated tick, because that target moves at the region's pace and held still it forecasts a body slowing onto a point the walk has already left. Accompanying declares itself a producer like every other request; before it did, the keep test flew whichever request had produced an earlier tick. None of it writes the navigator, the route or the hover. A tick with no forecastable producer holds its controls, which is the old reading. The straight-line reading is the dead end: in the third orb play a route running down along the top of a pool read as flying into the pool, the job was refused with no hit anywhere, and the choice that followed pinned the body to a slope for 2,491 ticks. The general form: a safety test run against a flight the body is not going to fly refuses the job for hazards the job avoids.

**A heading that takes the body nowhere is scored as the stop.** The contact holds a body out of a wall, so a heading straight into one reads safe and dry for the whole horizon while the body does not move, and interest then prefers it to every heading that moves — the other half of that pin, a body asking for full speed into a slope every tick. A candidate whose simulated body ends the horizon less than one body radius from where it began leaves the running and the stop stands for it, so it can never beat a heading that moves, and a tie is the stop's zero request rather than a shove into the wall. The radius is the floor because a smaller displacement cannot carry the body clear of anything, while a free burst from rest covers roughly ten radii over the horizon.

**Water and lava the body is not immune to are a mask, not a danger to trade against a hit.** Only the candidates that stay out of forbidden liquid longest are compared; while any stays dry for the whole horizon that is the dry set, so a candidate that reaches forbidden liquid is not a candidate and a body that cannot avoid both takes the hit. The danger reading is the hit alone and is read to the end of the horizon whatever the liquid did: the version before this cut a heading's safe ticks off at its first wet tick, which is the liquid-as-danger scale surviving inside the danger reading, and a body that started in water read every heading as hit at once, so interest alone chose and flew it into the shot.

Every tick's verdict is published as `CoordinateMovement.LastEvade`: whether the layer was off, kept the job, or bent it for a hit or for liquid, the horizon tick at which the job's own flight met each, the candidate chosen, and how many candidates were refused for liquid, for going nowhere and for danger. The recorder writes it at the end of the row. The contact the simulation runs pushes out of solid tiles only, so the first build scored against the contact alone and flew a body squeezed between two shots twenty-five pixels into lava; the second counted liquid as one more "unsafe at tick N", which held at the player's pace and failed at three times it, where the fleeing body outran one shot to the world's edge, found the lava a tick later than the shot and chose the lava. The general form: two outcomes that are not the same size of mistake cannot share one scale, because the scale then chooses between them by timing alone. NavReplay's evade row prints every tick's body, ask and bend around the first lava touch when it fails, and that trace is what found the corner. The answer bursts, so it turns away at the turn authority rather than easing. It replaced a reflex that took the body and a spacing search that held it, both of which suspended whatever the body was doing; nothing here touches the route, the activity or the hands.

A route is re-planned when the world is edited under it (the route keeps the box it was planned over and asks the edit record), when the body's immunities no longer match the ones it was planned under, when the segment ahead is no longer clear for the body, or when net displacement over a window stays under a floor — two such windows on one goal are two strikes, and the brain hands the goal back to the positioner, which bans the spot for a while. The status names the difference between a route being flown, a search still pending with the body following the route it had or steering straight where the line is clear, a proven absence, and arrival; every attempt ends completed, cancelled, pre-empted or failed, and the census counts places asked for against places reached so the record reads "asked for forty places, reached eleven" rather than a minute of following as thousands of requests. What planning cost is accumulated over every plan call and handed over by `TakePlanMs`, which resets it, so the one reader that asks once a row gets that row's own cost and a row with no plan reads zero; a last-plan figure left standing until the next plan made a sum over rows count one plan once per row.

## Traps

- `OrbPace` is written by the motor every tick from the live player and defaults to the fallback pace in `Selection/BehaviourWeights.cs`; a headless tool that never writes it runs the body a plain player would produce, and a fixture that changes the pace mid-run changes the braking distance the law reads next tick.
- The lookahead is the point drawn gold on the overlay and written to the record, and it is a steering target and not a waypoint: a body steering at it is often not heading at any point on the route.
- An evade that fires every tick a threat is merely near is a body that never flies its job. The predicate is a predicted collision within the horizon, never proximity, and the job's controls are tested before any heading is scored, so a threat the job's own flight already avoids costs nothing.
- The accompanying walk's parts interact, so a part is judged dead only against the final combination. The outward escape was once switched off with every accompanying row green and taken out, but that run still carried a target-motion rule that kept a refused body drifting; with that rule gone as well, a body under a block the player aimed at stayed on the tile for every tick of the courtesy scene and a passage walker met twelve stationary ticks. Mutate one part at a time against the tree that will ship.
- Two caps, and they are not interchangeable. The spot hover's is a drift's speed, because a body held at a tool stand or a firing spot should float about it; the accompanying pursuit's is the body's top speed, because the box it moves about travels with the player. Capping the accompanying pursuit at the hover's speed looks like a harmless unification and is the defect: at a running player's pace the body falls out of the back of the region and is sent home as outside, over and over.
