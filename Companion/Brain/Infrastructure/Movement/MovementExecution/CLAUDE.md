# Movement execution — committed traversals and local repairs share one controls contract

A traversal is one kind of move the body can make, and it is the answer to the defect that ran through every watched session of 2026-09-08: a companion standing still with a valid path in hand. Each time, the planner had proved a move by one rule (a body lowered row by row, a jump simulated with the follower's steering) and the follower had performed it by another (a steer to a tile centre, a jump at whatever speed the walk left), and the move that existed on paper did not exist for the body. So the two halves live in one class: `Candidates` proves every edge of that kind out of a node, `Steer` returns the controls that perform a step, `Done` says when it is reached and `Check` names why it cannot be, and both halves use the body in `../BodySimulation/` and the grid in `../RoutePlanning/` and nothing else. This is the navmesh-plus-character-controller split every engine ends up with, taken at the point where the two are one object rather than two teams' code.

## Step proof, step execution and the diagnostic capture

Diagnostic callbacks can retain the actual sampled local alternatives without running another search. Capture is opt-in at the call site, bounded by the inspector, and absent during ordinary play with the relevant layer disabled. `ProveInteractionJump` similarly uses the shared body model, but answers a work predicate: whether a dry ground jump reaches a supplied interaction and then lands safely nearby. It never writes velocity or grants an air jump.

An executable partial route is incomplete rather than failed, on both its first publication and later incremental replacements. An unfinished retained query without a prefix is pending rather than failed. Search status describes how much remains unknown; traversal outcomes describe what the body actually completed. A finished search with no usable prefix is a failed plan; retry cadence reads search completion independently of that reporting label.

`Navigator` lives beside the traversals and hands every step to the traversal of its kind. `Begin` runs once when the navigator starts the step, which is where a jump's run-up state is reset (keyed on the edge and not the path index, because a replan resets the index and a fresh path with a jump at the same index would otherwise inherit another jump's run-up). Every tick after that, `Done` is asked first, and the navigator advances past every step that is done; then `Check` is asked, and a fault (the body stood past its allowance, came to rest on a tile the step never promised, pressed a shape for too long, or the motion rule could not resolve where it is) ends the step: the navigator prices the step, counts a strike, asks for nothing until the next ground tick, and replans from there, so a step the body cannot take is replanned around instead of pressed against. Only then does `Steer` produce the tick's controls. The allowance is twice the ticks the step was proven to take plus a second, so a misland is caught by the landing and a park by the clock.

`PlanLocalMovement` owns the same `BodyState → Controls` contract for an uncommitted section: it scores a bounded set of controls from the body the engine actually left, including liquid state and remaining mobility, and rejects a prediction supplied as unsafe by a game-free threat oracle. It never replaces an active traversal's preparation controls. A running jump's back-off is part of the macro move it was proven as; choosing one tick only by proximity to the landing turns the body around at its runway mark. TraversalExecution supplies copyable policy state for full-macro simulation. A successful proof retains each control beside its expected pre-state and successor. Actual pose, velocity, contacts, mobility and terrain revision validate reuse; failed entries receive a proven preparation prefix or an immediate route rejection.

The rule that binds every subclass is that `Steer` uses the same functions `Candidates` simulated with: the jump's first tick steers toward the landing with `SteerToward` because the simulation's first tick did; the descent's controls are one function called by the simulation and the performer alike. Two consequences follow for the moves around a proven one. A coarse move proven from rest asks the preceding walk to coast onto its entry point (a walk done at ten pixels while still coasting handed the descent a body that slid off the lip at speed and hopped from the slope beyond it far enough to read as a fall); the actual-state proof validates the remaining entry offset and velocity before descent; and a move proven with a start speed (a running jump) exists only where the floor behind its take-off can actually build that speed, established by simulating the run-up rather than by measuring the floor against a formula — the section below on the three speeds says why the measurement was not enough — because the follow harness's first baseline (2026-09-08) found sixteen blocks whose only way out was a walk-speed jump off a slope with rock behind it, which the body could only attempt moving the wrong way; and the walk before a running jump arrives at that jump's speed and not the walk speed, because a half-speed arc taken at the walk speed flew into the fourth run's pool. The walk is the move that is proven twice, from rest and at the walk speed, because it is the one move the body performs at whatever speed the step before left it: where the two proofs land on the same row the walk is plain, and where they differ (a one-row ledge onto a lip the body at speed flies over) the walk starts from rest and the step before it stops. No subclass derives a move of its own any more, and the walk was the last that did.

**A reactive floor that runs where the planner has nothing to say is a rejected design, and the reason is not that it moved the body badly.** `ReactiveWalk` was the fighter AI's own obstacle ladder and gap leap, added on the argument that vanilla has no planner and is never frozen, so a failed plan should degrade to a zombie rather than to a statue. The argument survives; the mechanism does not. Its trigger was the absence of the ordinary path's precondition — no path, or a finished one — rather than the fact it existed to answer, which is the fallback failure this folder's Record already names in general terms, and a fallback triggered that way fires hardest exactly where the planner is still thinking. By the time it was removed the navigator had already grown the right answer beside it: where there is no live path, a bounded clearance search proves its own endpoint from the live body and the body holds when it cannot, which is a move the body can actually make rather than a guess that might work. The two had coexisted for long enough that the floor's controls were computed on exactly the ticks the navigator did not read them and read on exactly the ticks it did not compute them, so deleting it changed no behaviour at all — a fact worth recording, because a reader who finds the fighter-ladder idea attractive should know it was not removed for steering badly but for being the second owner of a question that already had an owner. Anything rebuilding it has to answer the trigger question first: what fact does this fire on, and is that fact "the planner failed" or merely "the planner has not finished".

**A performer must never answer a divergence by inventing a move, and the walk's own jump is the case that proves the cost.** `WalkTraversal.Steer` used to raise a jump for a wall ahead or a rise of two tiles or more, measuring that rise from the *live* body's bottom against the step's proven feet point. No walk edge can contain such a rise — the edge is proven by driving the body, and the only lift in that simulation is the motor's `StepUp`, which gains a tile at most — so the jump could only ever fire for a body that had already left what the proof described. The commonest way to be such a body is to stand on a floor slope, where the body rests partway down the diagonal and so sits low inside its own tile, which made an ordinary one-row step read as a two-tile rise. The walker then hopped it, and because it re-aims at its one-tile step every airborne tick, the steering reversed as soon as the body overflew it and braked the body to a stop on landing: the stop the hop was supposed to avoid. Measured on run 4's block 15, the lip at 3496,479 -> 3497,478 took 34 ticks against a proven 6, and the hop carried the body clean over the tile after it so that step never ran. Removing the jump outright took 27 of the corpus's 89 walked routes faster by 1,831 ticks in total with none slower, and dropped the follow pass's mislands from 13 to 8, because a hop that lands somewhere the plan never promised is a misland by another name. The answer to a divergence is the fault `Check` already reports — `Blocked` on a sideways press, `Stuck` on no headway — and a replan from the live state, which is the same answer every other divergence in this folder gets. `Tools/Scenarios/slope-lip-one-row-up-is-a-walk-not-a-hop.txt` is that lip cut out of the capture (83 ticks with the jump, 48 without), and the contract test in `Tools/NavReplay/VerifyMovementContracts.cs` asserts the property directly, so the guard does not depend on anyone reading a tick count.

## Entry preparation must prove the complete move from the actual body

Jump proposals ask the selected body backend for current gravity and adjust the nominal hop impulses to preserve their approximate heights. Actual-entry profile refinement asks the same question at the live body. The full impulse remains an alternative; the analytical apex only filters proposals, while complete simulated flight proves the landing. A fixed ordinary-gravity impulse under reduced gravity can exceed the bounded flight window even for a small ledge, making an executable low hop disappear from the graph. Enlarging the timeout alone leaves that mismatch in place. The native raised-lip fixtures cover both gravity regimes and both orientations; the candidate window and maximum proof duration remain bounded rather than claiming every physically possible sky jump.

A representative grid pose proposes an edge; it does not authorise executing that edge from a different pose or velocity. `PlanLocalMovement` proves the remaining macro from the engine's actual state. Its retained controls stay valid only while pose, velocity, contacts, mobility and terrain revision agree. The same threat oracle is checked afresh because enemies move independently of terrain.

If direct execution and profile refinement fail, preparation searches short control prefixes. A prefix is accepted only when simulating the complete intended traversal after it succeeds. Standing still without changing the entry cannot satisfy that condition. There is no stationary preparation timer to renew by choosing another destination. Preparation retains its controls and expected body states, so cadence replanning cannot interrupt a valid runway adjustment.

Physical rejection remains known across replacement execution objects when the step, next step, execution phase, body state and terrain revision match. Navigator excludes a rejected first edge only when its traversal declares that entry execution is independent of its successor; a walk's arrival speed depends on the following step and cannot be excluded this way. This memory belongs to physical entry, not the final destination. Announced terrain changes or materially changed body state invalidate it immediately. For unannounced sand and liquid changes, an aged rejection receives a fresh complete physical proof: only success removes the rejection, while a still-invalid move remains excluded. A threat-only rejection is never stored as a physical impossibility.

The initial search expansion generates profiles from the actual entry pose, bypassing the representative-node cache. Increasing preparation's prefix length alone cannot repair a proposal whose landing was generated from a different sub-tile position; the recorded offset-descent regression exercises this prerequisite. Later coarse edges remain proposals until their own actual-state proof succeeds.

The prefix search is bounded in simulated body ticks (`PlanLocalMovement.PrepareWorkTicks`), each prefix step charged one and each macro probe charged its own worst case, so the bound is a function of the move and the search shape and of nothing else; exhaustion records an unfinished search rather than a physical impossibility. The macro proof is bounded by its own tick limit alone. Neither reads a wall clock any more, for the reason above: a spent allowance exempts a refusal from its own strike, and an exemption whose trigger is how busy the frame was is not a rule. `PreparationMsBudget` survives as the clearance search's budget, which is a search over open space and is legitimately bounded by time. Headless tests starve the prefix search by setting the work bound rather than the clock.

**A sub-tile entry is a positioning problem, and a controller whose only outputs are full speed and nothing cannot solve one.** That is what the prefix search is doing for descents, invisibly: it accepts whichever short prefix makes the whole macro simulate clean, which is not a state any steering rule can be pointed at. Deleting it outright took the corpus from 5 mislandings to 44, almost all of them drops, across fourteen edges that had never mislanded — a rescued entry produces no fault, so the work it was doing is invisible in every measurement of failure. Two replacements inside `DropTraversal` were built and both failed on the captured sub-tile cave entry: steering onto the lip's point at the half-pixel tolerance the descent line is judged by cannot terminate, because `BodyPhysics.SteerToward`'s only non-zero output is the walk speed, so the body accelerates at a point it cannot stop within and hunts until the allowance runs out (33 faults and no arrival, against one fault and an arrival with no entry phase at all); creeping in at half the remaining distance per tick converges in principle and hunted in the same band in practice. What answers a body that is not where the proof stood is re-proving the move from where the body actually is, which the planner already does by expanding its first node from the live pose.

The historical brake experiment established the prerequisite for this design. Repeatedly braking while grounded made descent insensitive to small entry offsets but used different controls from its proof. Simply removing or latching that brake in the earlier resting-pose executor made 25 corpus routes faster, yet reduced completed routes from 67 to 63: four offset-sensitive landings or interrupted runways regressed. Actual-state macro validation is what changes that premise. `DropTraversal.Perform` now calls exactly the descent controls used by its edge proof, and invalid entries must obtain a proven preparation prefix or be rejected. Removing the brake without that validation remains a rejected approach.

## Falling through a platform means holding down, and the row to clear is the platform's

The game does not have a "drop through" event. `Collision.TileCollision` refuses to let a platform catch a body while the fall-through flag is set, and nothing else happens: passing a platform is the *absence* of being caught, tick after tick, for as long as the flag is held. Release it one tick early and the platform catches the body again, at which point the descent is back where it started and the whole thing repeats — which is a companion apparently frozen at a fixed pixel with a live velocity the collision engine cancels every tick, and is what three stretches totalling nearly seven hundred ticks looked like in the session of 2026-09-09.

The row that has to be cleared is the platform's, which is one below the lip the plan starts from. A body standing on a platform has its feet row at R and the platform tile at R+1, so a press released at `feetRow <= lipRow` releases after about two pixels of travel, still inside the platform's own tile, and the platform catches it. That off-by-one row is what the fix of 2026-09-09 corrected, and it was the *second* attempt on the same defect: the first moved the press off the on-ground gate and left the row wrong, which is why the arithmetic is written down here rather than left in the code.

**Holding the press is necessary and not sufficient, and the cause was our own motor climbing the platform back.** The playtest of 2026-09-09 at 13:46 recorded the press held for 211 of 265 frozen ticks — the column exists precisely so this is readable rather than inferred — with the body at a fixed pixel, its vertical velocity climbing 0.30 a tick to the engine's own fall-speed cap, and `collideX` and `collideY` both clear. Those cannot all be true of a body the engine is integrating, because `Collision_MoveWhileDry` does `position += velocity` unconditionally and sets a collide flag only when its tile collision changed the velocity it was handed. So something was writing the position back during the AI phase, and `moved` (`position - oldPosition`, with `oldPosition` assigned inside `Collision_MoveWhileDry` immediately before the integration) is blind to that by construction.

`CompanionMotor.ApplySteps` called `Collision.StepUp` with `holdsMatching: true` on every tick where `velocity.Y >= 0f`, which is every tick of a descent. Inside `StepUp`'s `flag4` that flag gates exactly one clause, the platform one — a solid block, a half block and a slope are accepted whatever it says — and `StepUp` writes `position` by reference while never touching `velocity.Y`. So the body climbed the platform it was falling through, every tick, with gravity accumulating underneath it. `Controls.Descend` now carries the descent's own vertical intent and `ApplySteps` passes `!descending`.

Four hypotheses are dead and are written as the property that failed, because each returns wearing new mechanics:

- **A press that is only renewed while the body is grounded cannot clear a platform.** True, fixed, and not the cause.
- **A press released at the lip's row releases inside the platform's own tile.** True, fixed, and not the cause.
- **The body was too wide for the shaft, or standing at a sub-tile offset the descent was not proven from.** False, and the arithmetic that killed it is worth keeping: the shaft at 3487,362 is three tiles wide and clear from pixel 55760 to 55808, the body is 20 pixels, and it sat with its centre at 55795, so its box spanned 55785 to 55805 with three pixels to spare — which is exactly the `hugRight` line the descent aims for. The claim came from reading the telemetry's `npc_px` as the box's left edge when it records `npc.Bottom`, whose X is the centre. A column's meaning is established from the writer before any geometry is argued from it.
- **The engine grants the fall and then undoes it in the same tick, through slope collision.** False on both halves. `Collision_MoveSlopesAndStairFall` does receive the fall flag (`if (fall) stairFall = true;` then `SlopeCollision(..., stairFall)`), so the earlier note claiming it was passed `stairFall` *instead of* the flag was wrong; and `SlopeCollision` only acts on tiles carrying slope ids 1 to 4, while the saved world shows shape 0 on every tile from x 3483 to 3491, y 358 to 371. `TileCollision` is exonerated too: its platform catch is fully disabled when both fall flags are set, and once the feet pass the tile top every remaining branch requires `!Main.tileSolidTop[type]`, which a platform fails.

**The lasting lesson is not about platforms.** The reason three attempts argued about the press is that the offline body and the game's body were never run on the same input and compared, so a shaft that replayed clean in 264 ticks and froze in play looked like a mystery instead of a disagreement — and the disagreement was one word wide. `BodyMotion.Step` gated its platform lift on `grounded`, which a falling body is not, so the offline rule happened to be safe; the motor gated its on `velocity.Y >= 0f`, which a falling body is. The standing hypothesis that the two differed over `PlatformTopCrossed` being edge-triggered where the game's platform catch is level-triggered (AIC-203 to AIC-206) is retired by that finding. A `diverge` column now measures the gap between the two rules live, every tick, on the real world.

## A profile names a speed; the floor behind the take-off decides it

A running jump is admitted, proven and performed, and for a long time those were three different speeds. The admission asked whether the floor behind the take-off could build the profile's speed *minus the slack the performer accepts when it crosses the take-off*; the proof then flew the arc at the full profile speed; and the performer, having backed away to its mark and run in along whatever floor was actually there, took off at whatever that run-up gave it. Where the floor was ample all three agreed and nothing was visible. Where the floor was a single tile against a wall they did not, and the gap was the whole defect: the planner offered an edge, the macro proof from the live body re-ran the same move through the performer and mislanded, and the step was refused before the body ever left the ground.

**An admission test that approximates a performance cannot stand in for running the performance.** The slack belongs to the performer's tolerance at the take-off line and never to the planner's question about whether a speed is reachable at all, and the two readings differ by exactly the slack — which over a thirty-tick flight is several pixels of reach and, on a marginal rise, the difference between a landing and the wall below it. `TakeOff` now answers the question by driving `Steer` from rest through `BodyMotion.Step` until it asks for the jump, and `Candidates` proves the arc from the state it returns. A formula over the runway length was rejected for the reason this whole folder exists: it would be a second rule, and the two would drift.

**The nominal speed then survived as the step's label, and a label a reader believes is a second rule wearing a disguise.** `NavStep.StartVx` held the profile's 3.5 while the arc had been flown at whatever the runway delivered, and every consumer of the field meant the body the floor cannot produce: the performer's commit test, its runway sizing, its allowance, and the walk that hands the body over. On the one-tile runway at 3392,627 the proof takes off at 1.68 and the performer took off anywhere between 1.31 and 1.98, because the commit was a lower bound on speed that a short runway can never satisfy and a latched run-up then overrode entirely, so the launch was whatever the six-pixel mark tolerance left. A third of a pixel per tick over a thirty-tick flight is the difference between the landing tile and the wall under it, and the 09:28 capture of 14 September holds 684 refusals against one jump fault with three edges carrying nearly all of them. The step carries `LaunchVx`, `RunUpBack`, `LaunchAlong` and `LaunchRise` now — the state the arc was proven from and the mark the run-up reached it from — and there is deliberately nowhere on a step to put a nominal.

Two properties hold that apart, and both are easy to lose. **The commit trigger is the proof's own rule and the entry band is a guard, never the trigger.** The proof is this code driven from rest at the node's pose, so it commits on the first grounded tick at or past the take-off; a performer that commits when the body enters the speed band instead commits on a *different tick* — one acceleration step before the clamp is a tick early — and two ticks apart is two launch states apart, which mislands however tight the band is. The band lives in `Check`, where a body that has run up, crossed the take-off and is not in the proven state ends the step with a fault the navigator prices. **And a run-in and a standing launch need different bands.** A run-in's lower bound on position is the crossing line itself, because a bound below it admits the tick the proof refused; a launch proven standing on the take-off gets a symmetric band round the proof's point, because giving it the run-in's rule leaves a body resting two pixels short of the line outside a band it cannot re-enter — the steer that would close the gap stops inside its own two-pixel tolerance — so the body pushes back and forth across the line until the allowance runs out.

The run-up's throttle is the proven speed, which makes it self-correcting: `BodyPhysics.StepVelocity` is the body's only horizontal rule and it clamps at its target, so a run-in from further back than the mark reaches the proven speed sooner and cruises at it, and only a run-in from nearer the take-off arrives under it. That is why the mark test accepts at-or-behind and nothing nearer. Finding that speed is an iteration rather than a lookup, because the step carries the proven speed and the proven speed is also the throttle: one pass at the nominal says what the floor delivers, the next asks the floor for exactly that, and a profile whose passes never agree is refused rather than offered at whichever pass ran last.

One exemption exists and it is not an optimisation. **A body already carrying the proven speed skips the run-up**, because sending it back to a mark is dangerous rather than merely wasteful: reversing from the walk speed costs eighteen ticks and about thirty pixels of forward coast, and a take-off is very often the lip of the gap being jumped, so the body walks off the edge it was standing on and falls. No control sequence rescues a body that close to a lip; what stops it arising is that the walk before a jump delivers the proven speed rather than the nominal.

Three consequences are worth knowing before touching it. A take-off is a function of the take-off tile, the profile speed and the body — never of the landing — which is what lets one run-up serve every landing tile in a direction, and the contract test asserts that independence rather than trusting it. A runway of zero is not a refusal: the performer jumps from standing where there is nowhere to back away to, so the proof simulates a standing-speed arc and files it under the running profile, which is exactly what the body does. And the graph gets smaller: across the committed corpus on 2026-09-11 the old rule proved 956,970 jumps of which 111,397 could not be flown by the performer, and the new rule proves 951,116 of which 152 cannot — a graph 0.6% smaller carrying 105,000 more edges that actually work. `--audit-jumps` is the measurement, and it needs no playtest.

One cost is recorded rather than hidden. Removing edges the body could never fly changes routes, and one corpus case (`2026-09-08_13-48-44-plans-shaped.txt#21`) went from walking a partial plan to its end to stranding at 3533,485 with a single walk edge and no route onward. Nothing physical regressed — those jumps never worked — but the new route descends into a pocket the graph now correctly reports as closed, which points at the one-way-drop policy rather than at the jump proof.

## The fact a guard answers is the fact it must test

A step the macro proof rejects before its first tick has failed as completely as one that failed in flight, and more cheaply, because the proof ran the whole move. Both guards that learn from a failure asked a proxy question instead, and both therefore never fired for a jump: the strike was gated on the attempt having ticked, which an entry rejection never does, and the rejection memory was gated on the traversal declaring its entry independent of its successor, which `JumpTraversal` inherited as `true` from a conservative default it never overrode. So the planner re-offered the same refused edge on every later plan, for ever — 246 times in the session of 2026-09-11, none of which the body flew.

The correction is the general one this folder's Record already states: **a fallback's trigger condition has to be the fact it exists to answer, never the absence of the ordinary path's own precondition.** The fact both guards answer is "the macro proof returned a physical fault", so both read that. The successor question moved to where the rejection is *consumed* — `EntryExcludedFromSearch` — because a rejection is a fact about the body and the terrain, and a guard that refuses to write the fact down because one consumer cannot use it leaves every other consumer with nothing. A threat rejection strikes nothing, because it is not a physical fault. A refusal whose preparation search ran out of its allowance strikes nothing and is not remembered, whatever the direct proof said, because the direct proof refuses only from the exact state and preparation is the question of whether a short run-in, stop or alignment makes the move; a spent allowance has not answered it, and removing that exemption costs what it was built to stop — a body reaching a ledge faster than it walks mislands going straight off and is rescued by shedding speed, so a starved allowance struck a reachable drop three to eight times per approach.

**That exemption was then reachable by ordinary running, which is how the circle came back, and the lesson is about bounds rather than about guards.** The allowance was two milliseconds of wall clock against a search that re-simulates a complete macro per candidate prefix, so it was spent on essentially every jump refusal: the exemption was not an exemption but the ordinary case, and a refused entry earned no strike, no memory and no controls. Beside it sat a cache of refused body states, which answered an identical state with its stored fault instead of simulating — and a refusal returns no controls, so the body does not move, so the next tick's state is identical by construction, so the cache's hit condition is a consequence of the thing it caches rather than a coincidence it exploits. Together they made a refusal permanent and silent at the same time: 684 physical-fault refusals against one jump fault in the census of the 09:28 capture of 14 September, 390 of them bit-identical on one edge. The cache is deleted and every refusal is proven again. The allowance is counted in simulated body ticks, because **a wall-clock bound on a physical question makes the verdict a property of the machine** — the same body on the same terrain refused on a busy frame and rescued on a quiet one, with nothing downstream able to tell which it got — and a verdict that exempts itself from its own strike is the last one to leave at the mercy of frame time.

The two guards bite at different ranges and that is worth knowing before relying on either. The rejection memory is keyed on the exact `BodyState` through `PlanLocalMovement.Matches`, so any motion at all erases it; it only holds for a body that is genuinely still. The strike is keyed on the step's tile and lasts a bounded number of ticks, so it is the one that ends a re-offer loop. Neither is visible in a recording on its own, which is why `BehaviourCensus` counts refusals per kind and per predicted fault beside the faults, and why `Tools/SessionReport/Checks/CheckPersistentRejections.cs` reads the god's-eye samples for one rejection repeating at one pixel: a refusal precedes the attempt's first tick, so it raises no fault, begins nothing and finishes nothing, and the navigator's `Rejected` status is overwritten inside the same brain tick — the 09:28 chronicle shows it on three ticks out of fifteen thousand.

## A held goal that is not delivered names the first contract that broke

`Navigator.LastFailure` is the navigator's explanation for a goal it is not delivering, and it exists because every failure signal this folder owns used to be read on its own — the search's stop, the macro proof's rejection, a fault, a strike — so a companion standing still was diagnosed by whichever signal a reader happened to open. The classes, and the evidence each is derived from, are the whole contract:

```
AbsentTransition     the retained or fresh search finished Exhausted and published no route
                     (a closed model, never proof the world is sealed)
UnfinishedSearch     a query still running without a route, one stopped on its work or deadline
                     limit, or a preparation search that spent its allowance
InvalidActualEntry   the macro proof from the live body failed physically before the attempt's
                     first tick and no refined profile or preparation prefix rescued it; or a
                     re-proof failed later while every observed tick matched its prediction; or
                     the world changed under an announced terrain callback mid-attempt
NativeMismatch       the attempt faulted after the observed body diverged from the predicted
                     successor with no external cause attributed
UnusableTerminal     body and proof agreed and the step still faulted on its own terms, or a
                     grounded body has no standable node under it
Preempted            another owner took the body (threat avoidance, a state search, downing,
                     recovery flight), an attributed external hit displaced it, or the threat
                     forecast refused the move
```

The navigator cannot see hits or terrain callbacks, so the coordinator hands it the motor's own attribution (`DisplacementCause`, from `CompanionMotor.DivergenceInvalidReason`) and the first cause seen during an attempt decides between mismatch, pre-emption and a refused entry. Without that input a knockback mid-jump reads as a native mismatch, and offline, where no motor exists, every divergence is honestly unattributed. What the class still cannot separate is an adapter defect from another mod writing the body's velocity; both are unattributed divergences and the class says so rather than guessing.

The verdict is sticky within a goal and yields in a fixed order. Arrival, a completed step, a voluntary release or a changed goal clears it. A search verdict replaces an older search verdict every time the search reports. A physical verdict holds against the replan that follows it — that replan often finds nothing precisely because the refused entry is now excluded — but only while no new attempt has begun and the terrain revision is the one it was found on, because an announced change makes the failure a statement about a world that no longer exists. Getting that last condition wrong kept a refused-entry verdict standing for 900 ticks behind a wall whose announcement had already made the goal absent.

Every begun attempt ends as exactly one of completed, physical failure, pre-empted or cancelled, and the navigator and the census count them apart. `Interrupt` takes the ending from its caller: `CoordinateMovement.Hold` is a cancellation unless it names the owner that pre-empted it, a state search pre-empts, a change from route to state search on one purpose is a cancellation, and a replan that replaces the step in hand is a cancellation. **Only a physical failure is evidence against a remembered connection**, so a knockback, a threat refusal or a preparation search that ran out of time no longer retires a route that works; a cancellation or pre-emption never did, and now an attributed external fault does not either.

The classification is descriptive. Strikes, rejection memory, spot bans and replanning read the same signals they read before; nothing is decided from the class except which remembered connections an attempt may retire. Goal-level no-progress (the partial-route alternation AIC-177 records) is not derived here yet: completed steps that never approach the goal still read as delivery.

## Search budgets, partial-progress commitment and retry cadence

Short grounded branches alone can exhaust a bounded search before any jump reaches its landing. A grounded jump therefore also offers its complete simulated flight, alongside the short intermediate branch. Search continues across a budget slice without committing a stationary or worse prefix merely because the slice ended. Landing, liquid-boundary crossing and actual goal satisfaction are meaningful prefix outcomes; height at a jump apex is not completion.

A local route repair requires endpoint satisfaction before executing its retained controls. The route planner owns strategic partial progress; accepting a second, Euclidean partial-progress policy after that route ends can undo a valid detour or drop into a neighbouring pocket. `allowPartialProgress` lets a state objective such as escape retain intermediate safe-state progress, while `Navigator` requires a complete local repair and otherwise leaves the body available for replanning or a different objective. A missing clearance proof is not a claim that the world is unreachable.

`SearchControlSequences` is the bounded local alternative when a route has no usable committed
edge: it searches the actual `BodyState` with the same `BodyMotion` backend, keeps a certified
prefix only while its predicted state, terrain revision and one-tick threat check still hold, and
clears its visited set when an exhausted frontier is reseeded from the live body. A jump apex is
not progress: non-goal prefixes are committed only at a landing or a liquid-boundary crossing,
because selecting an airborne lower-Y state repeatedly returned the full captured pool body to
the same submerged signature. The native full-capture replay in `EngineReplay --escape` exercises
production survival target selection and its real search budget; mirrored awnings exercise the
same generic rule in both directions.

The historical underground-house partial-goal fixtures still contain an unresolved progress failure: adjacent completed walks can alternate for thousands of ticks without a traversal fault. A per-edge success is not progress towards the final goal. AIC-177 retains those cases for goal-level progress accounting; the captured-entry fix and its no-stationary-preparation assertion do not establish freedom from every partial-route loop.

Every begun attempt has one terminal outcome. Arrival and replanning settle a completed step before replacing its policy state; interruption and clearing close an unfinished attempt. A fault already reported must not be reported again on the following clear. The census sizes its categories from the enums and counts interruptions separately from failures, because ordinary replanning is not a failed collision, and its endings section splits those interruptions into pre-empted and cancelled so the four endings sum to the attempts.

A movement allowance cannot belong only to an edge identity: a cadence replan can replace the edge before its allowance expires while the body never moves. The body-level stall monitor therefore reports a typed failure across replans, and it is the backstop rather than the answer — it fires forty-one motionless ticks after the refusal the step had already earned a strike for, so a refusal that reaches it has already cost the player most of a second of a companion standing still. **Failed physical proofs are no longer cached against their input state**, and the section above says why: repeating an identical rejected simulation does add cost without information, and skipping it made the refusal permanent, silent and unable to reach the preparation search that might have rescued it. `VerifyMovementContracts` asserts the re-proof directly, because the cheap version of this is the one that gets rebuilt.

**A retained search's retry decisions are measured from its answer, never from its question.** A body with no route to walk moves only by an endpoint-proven clearance, so standing still while the retained query is still running is waiting for computation: the stall monitor does not count it, and it earns no strike, which would otherwise force a fresh plan that discards the frontier and hand the brain a physical strike (two of which ban the spot) for a slow answer. A query that finishes without a route is not searched again at once from the same tile; the failed-plan retry waits from the tick it answered. Both used to restart the query from nothing whenever it needed more than a few dozen slices, which never finishes: 194 restarts in 8,000 starved ticks on a sealed corridor with the stall strike, 68 with only the immediate re-search, one after both were measured from the answer. Only a found route the body cannot join where it stands is re-planned immediately.

**The wait for an answer belongs to the goal, not to one search, and it is bounded.** The terrain revision is world-global, so any announced tile change anywhere invalidates a retained query, and `Plan` starts another unfinished one. An exemption scoped to the running query was therefore renewed by every restart: a body the terrain kept answering-in-progress, which is what a player mining nearby produces, never struck, never reached the spot ban and never fed regroup's travel pressure through `StuckTicks`. The navigator sums the ticks a route-less body has waited on its current goal's searches across every restart, and stops exempting it past `Weights.RouteAnswerWaitTicks`; after that the stall clock runs and strikes escalate exactly as for any still body. Only a moved goal, arrival, `Clear` or a route being walked starts the wait again, so an answer that arrives does not reset it and a second slow search for the same goal inherits the first one's wait. The bound's authority is the slowest legitimate answer measured, and it has to stay above that or a slow answer is struck and its frontier discarded. This also caps how long a pinned body with a pending search goes without a strike. Scoping invalidation spatially, so an edit outside a query's explored region leaves it valid, would cut restarts from distant edits and is not built; it would not remove the need for the bound, because a player mining inside the region still invalidates.

A failed attempt stays terminal until planning starts a fresh one, even when the edge is identical. Resetting its fault latch without reopening its outcome record makes the failure counter rise while the census remains at one. Navigator-local avoidance prices are scoped to its search with a finally block; appending them permanently to the caller's obstacle list makes retries progressively slower and prevents their expiry.

A new move (a second jump in the air, a dash, a swim, a wall latch, flight for a duration) is a new subclass and one line in `Traversal.Fresh`. Its `Candidates` simulates the move with `BodyMotion.Step` from the node's pose and yields the tiles the body ends on; its `Steer` produces the same controls tick by tick; and if the move depends on what came before it (jumps left, a cooldown) it reads and writes `MobilityState` on the body state, which the planner's node carries so two states on one tile are two nodes. The local executor, replay and reflex rollouts reuse those rules. The coarse graph does not yet enumerate arbitrary airborne ability chains; intermediate-state and resource-consumption tests are required before a mastery upgrade can claim them.
