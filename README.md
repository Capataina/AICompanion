# Multi... Player?

An AI companion for Terraria, as a singleplayer tModLoader mod. It is a small flying orb that travels with you, fights beside you with the weapons you hand it, drills the ore and trees you would, and joins nearby work. It is intended to feel like another presence playing the same game: free to stop for something useful, close enough to help when you need it. Independent gathering missions are outside that design; a mastery tree will expand its speed and toughness over time.

Early development, mid-rebuild: on the night of 14 September 2026 the player-shaped walking body was retired for the orb, and the tree is being rebuilt around it. `/companion` spawns a companion that follows you, hovers when idle, shares your max life, defence and mana, gets downed instead of dying (stand beside it for three seconds to revive, or wait ten for it to get up on its own), gathers reachable ore and nearby trees with a drill beam, and chooses attacks from the two weapons in its slots using predicted effective damage, threat removal and follow-up shots. Click its top-centre health notch for the native profile card: choose Off/Mimic/Auto mining and chopping, toggle voluntary hunting, pot breaking and torch placement, or step following distance through Close, Standard and Free. Drag the notch to move it; right-click it to reset. The card itself drags by its title bar, and Inventory and a selectable eight-branch mastery preview are pages within it, swapping the card's own body rather than opening another window. Mastery selections currently grant no stats or abilities and consume no materials. While a companion is up, enemies spawn twice as often.

Optional work can range beyond ordinary following and retains extra room to finish its current job and gather the drops. Permanent lighting uses Terraria's torch Smart Cursor eligibility and spacing, walks to the nearest proven dark-air site on screen, and consumes one torch from the companion's bag before using the player's inventory. Bed-containing rooms are protected from autonomous edits; open or oversized structures use conservative protection near the bed. This is early protection logic, not a general claim to recognise every player-built base.

# Expected Behaviour

This section describes the companion we are building, as a player would experience it. It is written as one continuous half-hour of play, from spawn to the first boss. It makes no reference to how any of it is achieved, and no reference to what the mod currently does. It is the target.

One idea generates all of it. **The companion is a competent friend playing the same game you are, at the same time, with its own opinions about what is worth doing.** It is not a pet, not a follower and not a unit you command. It hangs around roughly where you are because that is where the game is happening. It never waits for your permission and it never copies you. Its body is a small flying orb, a machine the size of a large fist, so it goes where there is room for it and nowhere there is not, and it uses the gear you hand it.

## Maintaining this section

**This section is the story; the table further down is the specification.** A behaviour is settled as a row in Behaviour By Behaviour first, and a scene here narrates it afterwards in a situation that could actually happen. So the question when editing is never "should this go in the story or the table" — it goes in both, in that order, and the scene is where a reader finds out what the row feels like at sixty frames a second.

**Nothing here may name a system, a score, a search, a mode or a behaviour-as-a-thing.** Write what the companion does and what it is weighing in the words a player would use. The failure is subtle because it reads as precision: "it checks every route before committing" sounds like a stronger sentence than "it makes certain it can get there, and where it cannot be certain it does not go", and it is a worse one, because it has decided that routes are checked and that there is a thing called a route.

**Nothing here compares against what the mod currently does.** No "this does not exist yet", no "this is the biggest gap", no "unlike today". The moment a scene knows what is built, it starts arguing with it, and this section has to stay readable as a description of a finished thing by someone who has never seen the code.

**A scene earns its place by containing a decision that could have gone the other way.** The pattern every scene follows: name the alternatives the companion actually weighed and rejected, not just the one it took; shift one thing mid-situation and show it adapting inside the same decision; add a branch that ends in a *different* action, with the reason; and close by naming the principle without naming a rule. A scene with one outcome teaches a rule and a scene with three teaches the thing that generates them, which is what this section is for.

**Prefer changing an existing scene to adding one.** The half-hour is a spine and it is more useful dense than long. A new behaviour usually belongs as a complication inside the scene it naturally collides with, and only earns its own timestamp when there is nowhere it fits.

**The body is an orb and no scene may need it to stand.** It hovers, drifts, dips and climbs; it never jumps, lands, walks up a stair or drops through a platform. A scene that finds itself wanting a floor under the companion is describing the old body and gets rewritten.

### 0:00 — You load in

The world loads on a clear morning and the companion is already in the air a few tiles off, a little above head height, turned whatever way it last looked. It does not snap round to face you and it does not park itself over your shoulder; it takes in what is around it — a few trees on the slope, nothing dangerous, nothing dark, nothing on the ground worth picking up — and having found nothing better to do, it stays near and idles. Idling is not hanging still. It drifts a little, sways when you sway, turns as you turn. The space around you is a place it is allowed to be, not a point it must occupy. When you set off to the left it comes with you rather than behind you: it is already on your left, a few tiles ahead and a little up, and it holds that side as you go. It is much quicker than you are, so being level or ahead of you is the ordinary case, and being behind you is what happens when it stopped for something rather than a policy of trailing. It moves the way a thrown thing moves, gathering speed, leaning into a turn, easing off as it arrives; it never snaps from one place to another. Getting where it was going is not stopping there, either. Once it arrives it keeps drifting a little around the spot, up and down and to either side, the way a hovering thing never quite holds still, so at no point in the whole half-hour is it a thing parked in the air. When it sets off again it gathers speed over a moment and curves into the new direction rather than cutting to it in a straight line, and however quick it is at full pace — still twice your running speed, and quicker still when your boots are — it is never so quick off the mark that it looks like it jumped.

While you stay on the quiet surface it wanders around you, dips to look at something on the ground, rises again and sometimes just hangs there. A useful tree further away can be worth the trip when nothing nearby needs attention, and the pull back toward you grows with how far that trip would take it: none while it is still in the space around you, more as the gap opens, and all of its attention only when it would otherwise lose you. Slimes trapped below a ledge do not change that by merely existing. A bat approaching you does: helping soon matters more than the same distant tree. If there is nothing useful further away either, it has no reason to disappear into an unexplored passage just to keep moving. And however far it ranges, it never climbs very far above you: a fist-sized thing thirty tiles up is a thing you cannot see, and it knows that, so it lives in the band of air a few tiles above your head and comes down to work.

### 2:00 — The slime that matters and the one that doesn't

A blue slime appears at the left edge of the screen, bouncing away from you, and the companion does nothing about it at all. It has looked at that slime and worked through it honestly: the bow you gave it cannot reach from here, and the only thing that would put it in range is a long flight to the left that ends with the companion thirty tiles behind you and a slime dead that was never going to reach either of you. It is not worth doing, so it is not done, and the companion does not fidget about it or half-start toward it. It also does not back away from that slime. Eight hops from connecting is not something that is about to land; treating it as if it were would mean flying the wrong way for no reason.

Put a bat at that same edge, with a clear line and nothing in the way, and the answer changes without the distance changing. The bat will be here in a second or two. That is coming, so the companion shoots it while still travelling. Put a slow, heavy thing there instead — something that will take five seconds to arrive and an age to put down — and that is a different kind of problem again: it is not about to hit anyone this instant, but leaving it means living with it, so helping with it matters more than the hop-count suggests.

Put the bat and the slime both on a path toward you. The bat will be here in a second, the slime in five, and there is not time to put both down before something lands. What it is weighing then is which hit it would rather take, not which creature is more of a project to finish. A tap on the slime, then the bat gone, then the slime finished, is an ordinary way to spend those seconds once both are in reach — not because it has a theory of knockback, but because the slime is not about to land and the bat is. What it never does is treat the longer fight as a reason to ignore the thing that is about to connect.

What it is weighing, across all of that, is how the thing actually moves, how soon it can really get here, and how much of a fight it would be, not whether something living is on the screen.

Change one thing and the answer changes with it. Stop walking, turn to a tree and start swinging, and the same receding slime becomes worth killing, because now the flight costs nothing — you are not going anywhere, and the companion will be back at your side before you have finished the trunk. Change a different thing instead: leave the slime where it is but have it bouncing toward you rather than away, and it becomes worth killing immediately, because it is going to arrive whether anyone goes anywhere or not. The companion draws and shoots it, and it does this while still moving with you, because aiming and moving are separate things and it has never had to stop doing one to do the other. Going toward a slime does not lock that slime as the shot: if on the way three bats line up, the shot it actually takes is the one through the bats, because that is the better use of the weapon from where it now is. Getting to a place a shot can exist, and choosing which shot to take, are different jobs. A weapon that would go through the wall does not need anyone to move; it just shoots. Where a wall does cut its line, it rises a tile or two, or slips sideways, and shoots from the new angle without breaking stride; you do not break stride either. What it never does is put itself between you and something to soak a hit; it is a fighter hovering where it can shoot, not a shield. Although, after you start breaking a second tree, it looks around and notices that the nearest threat, another slime, is so far that it would realistically not reach you any time soon, and the companion decides that instead of a grand repositioning to kill a "not so urgent" threat, it joins you on the wood.

Hand it a sword instead of a bow and the same slime is a different question. A sword means going to the slime, and the companion knows what that costs: it swoops in, hits, and pulls up and away before the slime can bounce into it, the way a wasp works a picnic. A sword against a slime that is coming to you anyway is a good trade; a sword against a slime thirty tiles away is a worse trade than the bow's, because now the whole trip is the price rather than the arrow. With both a sword and a bow in its hands, it takes the bow shot when a bow shot exists and closes with the sword when the thing is already on top of one of you.

### 4:00 — Trees, and reading where you are going

You walk past a single oak and neither of you stops, because one tree is not worth breaking a journey for. Two minutes later you pass a stand of four grown together, and this time the companion peels off, sinks to the base of the nearest trunk, and a beam comes out of it and eats through the wood in a few seconds; the tree comes down, it sweeps up the wood and the acorns, and it comes back — eight seconds gone and twelve tiles to make up, which it makes up in two. The extra wood is worth the short separation in this scene. Put danger ahead of you, or have you hurry away, and the same trees can be left standing. Their number alone does not settle the decision.

Later you hurry through a long tunnel without stopping and it lets ordinary work pass. You are covering ground, and repeatedly peeling off for ore would leave it spending the journey catching up even at its speed. Slow down and move around one chamber placing torches, and the answer changes: you are still active, but you are staying in the area. It can help with nearby ore without waiting for you to hold a pickaxe or start the same task.

There can still be time for a small job while you keep walking. A little exposed ore lies beside the tunnel, and the companion can drift over, drill it, collect the drops and comfortably catch you before the next bend. It stops briefly to do so. If you accelerate or the drops fall somewhere awkward, it leaves the rest and comes with you. The same judgement follows it into a fight or a dark chamber: useful help is weighed against how long you will be apart and what getting back together will require, rather than treating every pause as wrong or every nearby opportunity as worth taking.

While that is happening the ground ahead splits into a high ledge and a low path, both running right, and you jump up to the ledge. The companion simply rises with you: there is air above the ledge and it goes there, on your right, a little up, as if the ledge were not there. Then you change your mind, turn, and drop into a ravine, and the companion comes down with you straight away, ahead of you, because it read the turn and the drop as one movement and got there first. You think better of it and climb back out, and it is out before you are.

There is a way to get this wrong, and it matters that the companion does not. Reading where you are heading is not predicting where you will be in five seconds and racing there. If you are walking right and the companion is on your left, and you turn left for a moment to grab something, it does not go charging away to the left — it is already on your left, it is already where that brief turn was pointing, and a moment's turn is not a change of plan. It simply keeps moving with you, and when you resume right, nothing has been lost and no ground has opened up between you. It hurries only when it is genuinely far from where you are going.

There is a second way to get it wrong, and it is the one a fast body invites. Being quick enough to be anywhere is not a licence to be everywhere: it does not orbit you, it does not overshoot your every turn and swing back, and it does not hover exactly in the tile you are about to walk into. It settles a few tiles ahead on your side and a little above, and it stays there with small corrections, so that watching it is restful rather than frantic.

### 6:00 — The cave, and going in without you

There is a hole in the ground ahead and the inside of it is dark. The companion is aware of that darkness the way it is aware of the trees — as a place with a property, twelve tiles wide and unlit, sitting over there — rather than as something it notices only once it is inside it. Its light is off on the bright surface, because there is nothing here to light; the torch comes out as the light fails at the lip, and it goes away again the moment its surroundings are bright, whether the light is a torch it placed, one you are carrying, or the sun.

You keep walking along the surface and go straight past the hole. **The companion goes in anyway.** It drops in, lights the first chamber, and then, because the passage beyond it is dark too, it goes on and lights that, and the pocket off it, one dark patch after the next, and it comes back out to catch up with you only when there is nothing left down there worth lighting. At no point did it look to you for a decision. Had you gone in instead, it would already have been down there ahead of you doing the same thing. Keeping the place lit is its own responsibility and it discharges it whether or not you are participating; you have never once had to place a torch to make it place one.

This really highlights the agency of the companion. It does not simply follow you around; it makes decisions of its own. At this moment it weighed its options and decided that going into the cave to light it would be within budget and would not stray from what it thought your intended action was. Even if your intended action did stray from what the companion was doing, it was fine, because the companion weighs the options and decides that it has the time, regardless of what decision you might make.

### 8:00 — Crevices, and who gets the light first

If the last supplied torch has already been placed, it does not leave imaginary torches behind. It can still hold a light where that helps, and it makes the missing supplies understandable instead of repeatedly reaching for something neither of you has.

This time you do go down, and you head along a cave system that runs mostly sideways, with narrow side passages branching off it into the dark every so often. The companion ducks into them. It slips in, lights what is in there, and comes back out to rejoin you. It fits wherever you would fit and a little more, so a passage two tiles wide is a passage, and a crack one tile wide is a wall to it the way it is to you; it does not press at a crack and it does not try to squeeze. Because it flies, there is no such thing as a passage it can get into and not out of, so it never has to make the crevice-with-no-way-back judgement that a walker would; what it weighs instead is time, danger and how far you are getting.

Which dark it deals with first depends on you, and it reads that sensibly. If you are down here carrying nothing that gives off light, standing in your own small patch of black, then you are the dark that matters and the crevices can wait; the thing that gets someone killed is not seeing the skeleton beside them. If you are carrying a torch or you have thrown a glowstick ahead of you, your own bubble is handled, and the companion takes itself off on a proper expedition — the crevices, the corridor ahead, the chamber you have not walked into yet. Either way what it is pursuing is the same thing: that this cave is lit. And it means lit when you are gone, not lit while you stand there: the stretch of corridor that is bright only because you are walking through it with a torch in your hand is still on its list, and so is the ground under a light pet that will follow you out. Your light, its own light and the pet's light are all things that leave; a placed torch, a patch of lava and the sun are not, and it never confuses the two.

Partway along, the corridor runs into a wooden door somebody built into the rock a long time ago. It does not stop, and it does not treat the door as the end of the corridor — it opens it, slips through, and the door closes behind it, because that is simply what going through a door is. It is specific about which doors those are: one with rock hard against both sides cannot swing anywhere and is a wall wearing a door's shape, and the companion reads it as a wall and goes round; one with room on its far side opens and is fine. It plans on the answer rather than on the assumption, because pressing at a door that was never going to move is exactly the kind of thing this companion does not do.

Twenty tiles further on there is a fourth passage, and this one goes down a shaft to a pool. The companion does not go into the pool. Water hurts it — it is a machine, and it knows it — so the flooded part of the shaft is a wall to it as surely as rock, and it lights the shaft from above the waterline and leaves the drowned chamber dark. That is not a failure and the companion does not treat it as one or hover at the surface reconsidering. A passage lit down to the water is worth more than a companion sparking in the pool, and it makes that trade without hesitating and moves on.

Change what is in the passage and the answer moves again. Suppose there is something alive down there — a bat, say, awake and coming up toward the mouth. Now there are two reasons to deal with that passage rather than one, and both point the same way, so it deals with it: it backs off to where it has a clear line up the shaft and kills the bat on its way out, then lights what it can see. But suppose instead the thing down there is a jellyfish in the pool. Then the second reason points the other way from the first, because the only way to that jellyfish is through water, and it lights the shaft and leaves the jellyfish alone, and it is content to do that.

Each passage competes with moving with you, lighting the corridor ahead, dealing with the bat and staying somewhere safe. Darkness matters alongside the effort, danger and distance. The same passage can be worth entering at one moment and worth leaving alone a moment later because the rest of the scene has changed.

### 10:00 — Copper, and being sure

There is copper in the wall to the left, six tiles up, and the companion breaks off what it was doing and goes for it — not because ore is sacred, but because a vein it can actually get to is worth more than another torch right now.

It does not confuse seeing copper with being able to work on it. Where the way is clear it flies to the vein and hangs beside it, and the beam comes out. Copper behind solid dirt with no permitted way to reach it remains in the wall: it will not dig a tunnel to get there, and it will not pretend the dirt is not there. Copper across a gap is copper it simply flies to, and copper above a ledge is copper it simply rises to; the old questions of whether it can reach, climb or get back do not arise, and what remains is whether the trip is worth the time and the danger.

Once it is there it drills until the vein is gone, and when the last piece of it is out it dips and picks up what fell. You start on the other end of the same vein and it keeps helping. Copper you have already exposed or partly broken matters just as much as copper it worked on itself; what remains to be done is the useful part. Back among the trees it is different: while you cut one trunk, it usually chooses another, so the two of you make progress together without crowding the same tree.

On the way over it can shoot a passing enemy. Once it has started drilling, the beam stays on the work through the rhythm of the cuts. It does not sneak a bow shot into every pause and instantly turn the beam back on. A real interruption can make it stop the beam and fight, then return when that makes sense. Its torch is less demanding: it can lower the light to shoot and bring it back without turning that small courtesy into another job.

Getting out of the way is not an interruption at all. An arrow or a bat coming at it while it drills does not end the job: it curves off the line, lets the thing go past, and is back beside the ore with the beam on, the vein never having stopped being what it was doing. The same holds while it follows you or guards you — staying unhurt rides on top of whatever it is busy with, and it is never a second job that replaces the first. A companion that dropped everything and hid each time something moved near it would be a companion that did nothing else, and a zombie standing next to it is no reason to stay put while you walk away.

The next piece of copper falls off the far edge into a deep pit. Breaking it has not obliged the companion to follow it. A pit is not a trap for it, so what decides is only whether the trip down and back is worth the piece: if a fight has started or you are leaving the area, it leaves the drop below and moves on; if nothing else is happening, it dips, takes it and returns in a second.

There is more copper across the chamber, and this time the answer is no. Between here and there the chamber is flooded to the ceiling for six tiles, and the companion does not go through water. It does not fly to the edge of the water and hang there looking at it, which is the thing that would read as broken; it simply never chose that vein, so there is nothing to abandon and nothing to be stuck on. It goes and does the next best thing available, which turns out to be an unlit corner behind it, and the copper across the water stops being part of its thinking entirely.

Then you walk over and knock out two blocks so the water drains, and within a second or two the companion is on its way across. Nobody told it the world had changed and it was not sitting there hoping. It re-asks what is worth doing constantly, and what is worth doing is a function of what is actually there right now, so a wall coming down and an enemy arriving and a vein running out are all the same kind of event to it: the answer to "what now" came back different from the last time it asked.

The same thing runs the other way. Halfway to a vein it can reach, a skeleton walks into the corridor between it and the ore. The companion does not carry on to the vein and get hit on the way, and it also does not treat the interruption as the end of the job — it deals with the skeleton from above, where a skeleton cannot reach, and then, because the vein is still there and still worth having and it is still nearly on top of it, it carries on and drills it. Had the skeleton instead been three of them, and you in the corridor with them, the vein would have lost outright and it would have gone to you and fought from there. Neither of those is a rule about skeletons. They are the same question asked twice with different numbers in it.

The part it is strict about is the difference between *no* and *not sure yet*. Where the way is genuinely unknown — a dark stretch it has not looked at, a region it has not worked out yet — it does not fly at a maybe; it does something it already knows it can do and looks again a moment later. A new opening or an upgrade that lets it touch water can change the answer later, so leaving the vein alone now does not mean forgetting it forever.

### 12:00 — One thing after another

You are at a chest and stationary. Ahead of the companion, in a line, there is a pot on a ledge, an unlit corner above the pot, and a vein ten tiles past both. It wants the vein, and getting to the vein takes it right past the other two.

So it breaks the pot on the way, with whatever weapon it has — an arrow if it has a bow, a swipe if it has a sword — because the possible contents are worth the small effort here. The contents drop and it scoops them up without slowing. The corner is a tile above the line, so it rises and lights it. Then it arrives at the vein and starts drilling. Each small diversion was worthwhile because the journey was already being made. If the vein is gone but the pot is still nearby, a short trip for its possible contents can be worthwhile on its own. If those contents would fall into water, or its bag is full, the same pot can be left alone. Terraria is a game of doing three things on the way to a fourth, and the companion plays it that way while remaining free to change its mind.

The same idea decides which way it goes at all. Off to the left there is a single slime and a drop. Off to the right there is one enemy and one unlit corner. Straight ahead there is a stretch with four dark spots, two pots and something alive in it. It goes straight ahead — not because lighting is more important than killing, and not because pots are more important than slimes, but because that is where the evening's work is sitting together. Put the same four dark spots behind a flooded stretch, and it takes the smaller place it can reach. Start walking away while it is in there, and catching you up can beat finishing every last corner. The place with more to do is worth more only while getting there, doing it and getting back still makes sense.

Halfway through the vein a bat comes in through a gap in the ceiling and makes for you. The companion stops mid-cut — the cut is not sacred either — kills the bat, and goes straight back to the same vein and finishes it. That return is seamless. It does not hang there reconsidering its life, and it does not wander off to something else on the way back; the vein was the best thing available before the bat and it still is.

It matters what it does *not* do on that trip out. It does not pass the pot and make an unnecessary second journey for it later. It does not return to you between every small action. And being interested in ore does not make a useful nearby pot or dark corner somebody else's problem. Those diversions remain choices about what they add to the journey, rather than obligations to collect and finish everything it has noticed.

Move the pot and the answer changes without anything else changing. Put it twenty tiles off the route and the companion passes it by — not because pots stop being worth breaking at some distance, but because that pot now costs a trip and a trip back and the ore does not. Move it back onto the path and it is worth it again. The unlit corner behaves the same way: a tile above the route it takes it, twenty tiles off to the side down a dead end it leaves it, and if you stand in that dead end a moment later it becomes worth lighting again because now there is a second reason.

Change the timing instead of the geometry. If the bat had come in while the companion was still on its way to the vein rather than mid-cut, the pot and the torch would simply not have happened — it would have turned and dealt with the bat from wherever it was, and picked the journey up afterwards with whatever was still on it. If the bat had come in and you had killed it yourself before the companion got there, it would never have broken stride at all. And if by the time it finished with the bat you had walked forty tiles further on and the vein was now well behind it, the vein would have lost to catching up with you and it would have left the copper in the wall without any sense of having failed at something.

Change how much work remains. With five pieces of ore left and a dangerous zombie approaching, it stops the beam because finishing would take too long. With one easy piece left and the zombie still distant, it can finish before the trouble arrives. Give both of you enough protection that the creature would barely scratch you, and there may be no reason to interrupt at all. Send a serious threat towards you instead and helping you can matter more than the nearly finished ore. The difference is what another moment of work will cost, not pride in how much work it has already done.

### 14:00 — The slimes it was ignoring

Three slimes have been audible for a while in a chamber off to the side, and the companion has done nothing about them the whole time, because they cannot get out and it has been busy with things that were worth more. Now the vein is gone, the drops are collected, the corner is lit, there is nothing else on the floor, and you are still standing at your chest. With everything better used up, the slimes are now the best thing there is, and the companion goes and deals with them.

It gets itself somewhere it can shoot from — above them, out of a slime's reach, which for a slime is nearly anywhere — and starts putting damage in. They bounce at it, and it drifts up and back as they come, and it kills all three without taking a hit. It collects what they drop. Then it looks around again, finds a lit cave with nothing on the floor, no ore, nothing alive and a player who has not moved, and comes back to hang near you. Nobody told it to do any of that in any particular order; the order fell out of what each thing was worth at the time.

The interesting thing here is the two minutes when it did nothing about them, because that is the part that usually reads as broken and is in fact the point. It was aware of those slimes the whole time. It did not forget them, it was not unable to see them, and it was not waiting for a signal. They were simply worth less than a vein of copper and less than an unlit chamber and less than staying near you, and a thing worth less than the thing you are doing is a thing you do not do. The moment everything above them was gone they rose to the top without any change in how it felt about slimes.

Change the circumstances and the timing moves. If one of those slimes had found its way out of the chamber and started bouncing toward you while the companion was still drilling, it would have left the vein for that one and gone back to the vein afterwards — not because a slime outranks copper, but because a slime that is arriving is a completely different thing from a slime that is sitting in a sealed room. If you had got up from the chest and started walking on while it was fighting them, it would have finished the fight only if finishing was quick, and otherwise broken off mid-fight and come with you, leaving two slimes alive in a room it has no further interest in. And if the chamber had been sealed in a way it could never open, the slimes would have stayed at the bottom of its list for ever and it would have gone and found something else to do rather than pressing at the wall.

### 16:00 — Everything at once

Terraria does not hand you one problem at a time, and this is the part that matters most.

You are up on a dry ledge on the left. Below and to the right is a water pool with two jellyfish in it, there is unmined ore in the floor of that pool, two zombies are closing from the right on the far side of the water, and the whole area is half-lit. The companion was drilling the ore from just above the surface of the pool, as close as it dared, when a jellyfish surged and clipped it into the water, so it is now in the pool, sparking, within reach of two jellyfish, with zombies inbound and you dry and safe twenty tiles away.

It does not freeze and it does not try to finish what it started. It leaves — straight up out of the water, then to the left, up to the air over your ledge — and it does that not because you are there but because that is the only place in this scene that is not actively costing it. Hanging over the ore means being hurt by the water and bitten by two things, and a copper vein is not worth that. Once it is out and dry, the jellyfish stop being a problem at all, instantly and completely, because jellyfish cannot leave water and the companion is aware of that. It turns and starts shooting the zombies as they come, giving ground to the left to keep the gap open.

Then a bat comes in from the left, behind it, and it is pinched. What it does here is the whole character of the thing. It kills the bat first — not because bats are more dangerous than zombies, but because the bat is the one thing in this scene that can actually reach it in the air, and a zombie underneath a flying orb is a zombie that will never connect. Set the same scene slightly differently — the bat still distant, the zombies about to reach you — and it kills the zombies instead, because now you are the one about to be hit. Same reasoning, opposite action, and nothing in it was decided in advance.

Put a zombie between the companion and you, and helping you does not mean flying through it: it goes over, which for the companion is simply the way from here to there, and shoots down on the way. If there is no useful intervention from safety, it weighs the real damage and the danger you face rather than treating your position as somewhere it must reach at any cost.

With the bat dead and the zombies down it hangs above the pool and shoots the jellyfish dead from the air, dry and out of reach the whole time. With the pool safe it drops back to just above the surface, drills the ore it came for in the first place from there — leaning into the water is not the same as entering it, and the beam reaches — and collects the lot on its way back up: jellyfish drops, zombie drops, the bat, the ore. Then it comes back to you.

Three things it did not do are as important as what it did. It did not finish the ore while it was in the pool. It had wanted it thirty seconds earlier, and it left it — because a vein is worth a fixed amount and sparking in water being bitten is not, and no amount of being partway through something makes finishing it worth more than it is worth. It did not run to you for safety, either; it ended up over your ledge, but it went there because that air was dry and out of reach of everything, and it would have gone to an identical spot in the opposite direction just as readily. And it did not stop shooting while it was retreating. Climbing out of the water, it was still putting arrows into whatever it had a line to.

Now change the water. Make the pool shallow and the ore exposed on a dry lip beside it, and most of this scene evaporates: nothing hurts it there, so the ore is worth finishing, and the companion stays, kills the jellyfish from where it hovers because they are now the nearest thing that can hit it, and drills. The zombies arriving change that only when they arrive. Give it the upgrade that lets it touch water and the pool stops being a wall entirely: it goes in, drills from inside, and the jellyfish are just two more things to shoot.

Change who is in the water instead. If *you* had dropped into that pool with two jellyfish and the companion were dry above, it would not go in after you. It would shoot the jellyfish from the air, which is where it can hit them and they cannot hit it, and it would do that faster than it does anything else, because the thing hurting you outranks the thing hurting it. Joining you in the water would put a second body in the same trouble and remove the only angle from which the problem could be solved.

And change the bat's arrival to a moment later. If it had come in after the zombies were already dead, the companion would simply have shot it from where it hung and gone back to the ore, and none of the climbing and repositioning would have happened at all. Nothing about the bat decided that sequence; the sequence came from the bat arriving at a moment when it was already backing toward where the bat was. That is the character of the whole scene — every move in it was the best available answer to a question that was different one second earlier.

### 18:00 — The shaft it should not have entered

You jump down a shaft and it comes down with you. Halfway down it registers what is at the bottom: water, right across the shaft, and you are going to land in it. It stops. It does not follow you into the pool, because the pool would hurt it and would not hurt you, and it hangs in the air above the waterline where it can still see you. If something is in the water with you it shoots that from above; if nothing is, it waits, and it lights the shaft while it waits, because a dark shaft with a player at the bottom is a dark that matters.

That is the load-bearing half of this scene. What the companion is willing to go into is a function of what its body can currently survive, and that changes as it grows. Early on, a flooded shaft is a wall and it stops at the waterline. Once it has earned the upgrade that lets it touch water, the same shaft is fine, and it comes down into the pool with you and works there as if it were air. The judgement did not change and the shaft did not change; the set of things it can survive changed, and every decision that rests on "can I be there" quietly moved with it. It should never be the case that it has an immunity and does not use it when choosing, or that it plans as though it has one it does not.

If it is knocked into water anyway — a bat clips it, a jellyfish surges — it does not stay and reason about it. It goes up. Up is always the way out of water for a thing that flies, and it takes that way immediately rather than being hurt while it works out what to do; only if something above is worse does it go sideways instead, and only if it is boxed in on every side does it stay and kill what is biting it, because then the only way to stop being hurt is for the hurting to stop.

Its growth follows yours as well as its own practice. When you become faster it becomes faster in step, so it is never left behind by a new pair of boots; when you gain more reach it drills from further; when your defence rises so does its own. It uses the extra speed and reach in ordinary decisions immediately: a higher piece of ore can be worked from further off, a torch can go somewhere it could not before. Better protection also changes which small dangers are worth interrupting work for. Losing an advantage matters just as promptly; it does not attempt yesterday's flight with today's slower body.

### 20:00 — The wall of lava

You are working along a tunnel and it opens into a chamber whose floor is lava, with ore glinting in the wall on the far side. The companion looks at it and does not go. Lava hurts it as water does, and the ore is only reachable by crossing above a lake of it where one bump from anything would drop it in. It weighs the ore, the danger and the way back, and the ore loses, and it says nothing about it and goes on to something else.

Change the geometry and the answer moves. If the ore is reachable by a route that keeps rock under it and the lava well below, it goes, because now the lava is scenery rather than a price. If the lava is a thin stream it must cross in one clean pass with nothing alive nearby, it crosses, high and fast, because a two-second crossing with nothing to knock it down is a cost it can afford. If a bat is circling over that stream, the same crossing is a different trade and it waits for the bat to be dead or gone.

Knock it into the lava anyway — a shove from something you did not see — and it does not stop at being hurt. It goes up, out of the lava, in the first fraction of a second, the same as water, and only then looks around. Give it the upgrade that makes lava harmless and the whole chamber turns from a wall into a room: it crosses the lake as if it were floor, drills the ore, and comes back over the lava with the drops, and nothing about what it wanted had to change for that to happen.

The thing it must never be is idle and in danger at the same time. Being hurt and being out of things to do are two separate facts, and it only says the first one out loud once it has already got out.

### 22:00 — Somewhere it cannot follow

You take a route it cannot take: a swim under a flooded wall, a dive to the bottom of a lake. It does not hang at the waterline trying the same impossible move. Useful things remain nearby — trees, a slime, drops on the floor, a dark corner — and it can get on with them while you are still close enough that this makes sense. Distance matters throughout, alongside danger and the work remaining. If you keep moving away underwater, rejoining you can become more important while trees are still standing, and it tracks you along the surface as best it can; if you remain nearby, it need not abandon useful work merely because it cannot be beside you.

The clearest version of this used to be the floating island, and for the companion the island is not a problem at all: you fly up and it flies up with you, and if it is a long way it takes a moment longer and arrives. What it cannot follow is the water, and only until it has earned the upgrade. Where there is genuinely nothing on this side of the water — a bare stone corridor with no trees, no enemies, nothing on the floor and nothing dark — then the distance to you is the only thing left on the table, and it acts on that immediately rather than after a delay, waiting as close to you as the water allows. There is no pointless pause before it tries to rejoin you. "Nothing to do" and "go to the player" are the same answer arrived at from two directions.

An enemy arriving changes it back. Something wanders into the corridor while you are still underwater and the companion deals with it, then returns to the question. That is not it forgetting about you; the distance to you was the best thing available for as long as nothing better existed, and something better existed for thirty seconds.

### 24:00 — Home, and getting out of the way

You head back with a full bag and it comes with you, gathering useful drops along the route while it has room. Even a single block of dirt can be worth a two-second dip: it need not know the importance of every item to your playthrough to help carry it. Put that same block at the bottom of a pool or hurry away from a fight, and it leaves it behind. Giving ordinary items a chance is different from promising to collect every one.

When your inventory cannot take a drop, its own bag can carry it. When neither of you has room, it leaves the item where it is and makes the lack of space clear; the item does not vanish and the companion does not keep making the same fruitless trip. Free some space or change what lies nearby, and collecting can become useful again.

At home you start sinking a shaft — the two-wide platform drop straight down — and the companion is hanging exactly where the next platform goes. It moves. While you are holding something you can place and your cursor is anywhere near it, that cursor is a place it stays out of, so it drifts up, or sideways, and it keeps doing that as you work your way down the shaft, out of the way each time before you click rather than after.

Swap what is in your hand and it stops caring. Holding a sword, a pickaxe or a torch, it hangs wherever it likes, because none of those are blocked by a body being in the way and getting shoved around by your cursor for no reason would be its own kind of annoying. The thing it is reacting to is not your cursor; it is the fact that you are about to put something where it is.

The same courtesy is wider than building. It is never the thing hanging in the one-tile-tall gap you are walking through — it is above the gap or through it first — and in a doorway it goes through ahead of you or waits on its own side, rather than meeting you in the frame. At home it comes in through the door with you and finds a corner of the room to hang in, out of the line between you and your chests. None of that is a set of rules about doorways and shafts; it is one idea — being where you are trying to be is a cost — applied everywhere it comes up.

There is a version of this that would be worse than not having it. If it took the idea too seriously it would spend the whole session skittering away from you, unable to hang anywhere because you might want to be there. What stops that is that being in your way is a small cost rather than a prohibition: it is enough to move it out of the tile you are about to fill, and nowhere near enough to make it abandon a good firing position because you glanced in that direction.

### 26:00 — The eye

Night falls and you use the Suspicious Looking Eye. The music changes and the Eye of Cthulhu comes down out of the dark.

Everything else the companion was interested in stops mattering. It is not drilling, it is not lighting anything, it is not sweeping the floor, and it is not hanging near you — because nothing else in the world is worth anything while this thing is alive. It has two concerns now and they are in a strict order: **do not get hit, then do damage.** In that order, and the order is not negotiable. If the only way to get the boss in range is to fly somewhere it will take a hit, it does not fly there; it stays out and does less damage, because it cannot do any damage at all once it is on the floor.

It also stops trying to look after you, and that is correct. Ordinarily, when something comes for you, the companion makes a judgement — that zombie is heading for him, but he can handle a zombie, so I will finish this vein and step in if it turns serious. Against a boss that judgement is meaningless, because there is no version of this where it hangs between you and the Eye of Cthulhu and that helps. So it drops the idea entirely and fights.

It also stops being tethered to you the way it normally is. Boss arenas are enormous; a companion held on a short leash spends the whole fight bobbing around your shoulder instead of fighting. It ranges much further than it ever would ordinarily, and higher, with a gentle pull back that gets stronger the further out it goes, so it has room to manoeuvre without ever genuinely leaving. The same is true whenever the game itself turns into an event — a Blood Moon, a Solar Eclipse, a Goblin Invasion, a Pumpkin Moon — because those are the same situation: the world is now about one thing, and hanging next to you being helpful is not it.

What it does not set down is you being in trouble for reasons it *can* act on: if the small eyes swarm you specifically, they are things it can shoot and they are on you, and it shoots those before it goes back to the boss. Nothing else survives the fight either. It does not drill the arena wall because there is copper in it. It does not stop to light a dark corner. It does not pick up the drops until the fight is over — they are not going anywhere, and a detour for loot mid-fight is how a fight goes wrong.

If you die halfway through, it does not stop fighting and it does not hang over your gravestone. The boss is still alive, it is still the only thing that matters, and it keeps going for as long as it can — which is often long enough to matter, because a boss that is nearly dead when you respawn is a very different fight from one at full health.

Take the same evening and replace the boss with a Blood Moon and very little changes. There is no single enormous thing to shoot, but the world is still about one thing, so the companion is still fighting rather than drilling, still ranging further than usual, and still prioritising its own survival over the last few points of damage. What tells it this is happening is the state of the world rather than the identity of the monster, which is why an invasion from a mod it has never heard of produces the same behaviour.

### 28:00 — The fight

It fights the way a decent player with wings fights. It stays at the range where it can hit and not be hit, and it keeps moving. When the Eye stops circling and lines itself up for a charge, the companion is out of that line before the charge lands — not by recognising this particular boss, which it has never heard of, but by seeing something aiming itself and accelerating and getting off the line it is aiming along. Off the line, for a thing that flies, is usually up or down rather than back, because back along the charge is the one direction that does not work. That works the first time it meets an attack nobody has ever described to it.

When the Eye splits and starts throwing out a swarm of small eyes, it changes weapon, and it changes weapon for the right reason. Something that punches through a line of targets is worth far more against a swarm than against one large thing, and something that hits one target very hard is the reverse. It is looking at the two weapons you gave it, the targets and how those things are arranged. Hand it a different pair and the same judgement considers what those two can actually do. It does not take a weapon out of your inventory and pretend to know how it works: what it uses is what you put in its hands, and it learns each one by using it — the first few shots with a new weapon can go wide while it gets the feel of how that thing flies, and after that it aims like it has always had it.

A staff in its hands is the same story with one more thing to watch. Magic tires it: the more it casts without a pause, the weaker each cast lands, down to about half, and it never refuses to cast. So a staff that started the fight as the better weapon can become the worse one twenty seconds in, and when it does the companion swaps to the bow, not because a rule said so, but because the bow is now the weapon doing more. Give it a moment and the staff comes back.

Its own survival keeps winning ties all the way through. At full health with a clear line it is aggressive, sits at the edge of its range and fires constantly. At a third health it is noticeably more careful — wider standoff, quicker to break off, more willing to spend a second getting somewhere safe rather than squeezing in another shot — not because a threshold was crossed but because getting hit costs more when there is less left. If you are at full health and it is nearly dead, it looks after itself, because you can take a hit and it cannot, and a companion that dies to prove a point contributes nothing for the rest of the fight.

If it does go down mid-fight, it is back in the air within ten seconds and rejoins, so a bad moment costs you its help for ten seconds rather than for the rest of the fight.

### 30:00 — After

The Eye dies and the loot scatters across the arena. The companion sweeps down, collects what it can reach and brings it over.

The return to ordinary is immediate and complete. There is no cooldown, no winding down, no hanging still for a moment — the enormous thing that was outweighing everything else simply is not there any more, and the next-best thing on the pile takes over, which in a boss arena at night is usually the loot and then the fact that half the arena is unlit. Within a few seconds it is a companion again: hovering near you, looking around, and if there is a tree or a dark corner or a vein anywhere close, it has already gone to deal with it.

Summon the next boss thirty seconds later and it drops everything again just as fast, mid-cut if it is mid-cut. The unfinished work can wait. When the fight ends, it returns to whatever is then useful, without a needless pause or an obligation to recreate the interrupted evening.

That is the whole character of it: useful intentions without stubborn obligations. It can remember the vein it left, finish work that is still worth finishing, and abandon it when the world has changed. It acts coherently enough to look deliberate and adapts readily enough to remain helpful. How it reaches those decisions does not change what the player should experience.

# The System In Place

This section describes what is actually built, read from the source. It says what each part is for, how the parts hand work to each other, and where the recordings show a gap between intent and result. It describes the machinery; it does not defend it.

Source assessment: **15 September 2026**, source at `52c0576` and the review fixes after it, the tree built after the first play of the orb. That play, 08:30 on packaged 0.26.0, is in Current Behaviour and shows the body before the change this section describes: since then the orb hovers wherever it is asked to be instead of stopping, eases into and out of every move at the same top speed, and bends its flight away from a predicted hit instead of handing the body to a safety response, and none of that has a capture yet. So this section is read from source and from the headless suite, and the suite establishes its fixtures, not every cave. The one measure it holds of the orb against real play is that recorded route, on which the orb reaches all twenty places the player stood, its mean distance from the recorded companion's track a fifth shorter than the walker's; that is a headless replay of a player track and not a play, and the play is what comes next.

## Maintaining this section

**Update it in the commit that changes the code, never afterwards.** A description of machinery that has moved on is worse than no description, because it is believed. If a commit changes what a part does, changes what owns a decision, or removes something named here, this section changes in the same commit.

**Write the shape of a rule and never the value that fills it in.** "The running behaviour keeps a bonus so a near-tie does not oscillate" survives every tuning session; "the running behaviour is multiplied by 1.15" is falsified the first time anyone touches the constant, and nobody deletes prose when they edit a number. The same goes for budgets, radii, thresholds and timers: name what the number is for and where it lives, not what it currently is. A number stays only when it is a fact about the game itself, a structural count that could only change by redesign, or a dated measurement that says it is one.

**Read the source, not the folder notes.** The per-folder CLAUDE.md files are maintained by hand and can lag. Anything asserted here about how a system behaves is checked against the code that runs, and a claim that came from a comment is either verified or written as a claim the comment makes.

**Say what a part deliberately does not do.** Half the value of this section is stopping the next reader proposing something that was already considered and rejected — so a rejected approach is recorded as the property that failed rather than the patch that expressed it, because a design that comes back wearing new mechanics will otherwise pass unnoticed.

**When this section explains a defect, the explanation lives in the table's System column and is referenced from here, not duplicated.** Two copies of one diagnosis drift apart, and the table is the one people act on.

## The whole system on one page

The companion runs one short loop sixty times a second.

1. **It looks.** The game hands the brain what it can observe: where you are and how you have been moving, which enemies and projectiles are near and whom they can reach, what drops are lying around and whether the bag has room, how bright the open air is wherever the game has actually computed light — less every light somebody is carrying, so a passage beside you holding a torch does not read as a lit passage — and the companion's own health, mana and surroundings.
2. **It checks whether it is in trouble.** If it is touching water or lava, a shared safety system takes the body out of it. Ordinary work pauses; shooting can continue. Being about to be hit takes nothing: it is noted here, and step 5 bends whatever the body was asked to do out of the way.
3. **Otherwise it decides what is worth doing.** Seven behaviours, grouped into three families, each look at the world and say how much they are worth right now and what they would go after. Each family puts forward its best behaviour. The three offers are compared by the same rules, which account for danger to you, how long the job would keep it away, and how hard it would be to get back to you. The best offer runs. Staying with you is one of those offers, not what happens when nothing else does.
4. **The winning behaviour asks for a kind of place** — next to you, beside an ore it can reach, somewhere it can shoot from — and position selection turns that into a point in free space to hover at. Where the body can fly to is one flood over the free space around it that the positioner, the tool work and the lighting all read, so a place nobody has worked out yet is unknown to all of them rather than absent from one of them.
5. **Movement gets it there** with the same body rules for every purpose: a route through free space wide enough for the body, steering that carries momentum along it and eases in, a slow hover around the place once it is there, a bend away from any hit the flight is about to take, and one motor that is the only thing allowed to move the NPC and the only thing that keeps it out of walls.
6. **The hands act on their own.** The two weapons you handed it pick a worthwhile target between them and fire whenever the hand is free, whatever the body is doing. Which weapon and which body are one choice, because a piercing shot through three things can beat a harder hit on one. Flying toward a creature does not pick that creature as the shot. The pickaxe and axe you handed it keep the hand for the whole swing cycle. A torch fills an otherwise empty hand in the dark.
7. **It watches what actually happened** — whether the body moved, whether a block took damage, whether an item reached the bag — and the next loop starts from that, not from what it hoped would happen.

```text
   world ──► observe ──► shared safety? ──yes──► leave water or lava ─────────┐
                              │ no                                           │
                              ▼                                              │
                 prepare 7 behaviours (3 families)                           │
                              ▼                                              │
               each family nominates its best behaviour                      │
                              ▼                                              │
               compare the 3 offers ──► winning behaviour                     │
                              ▼                                              │
               ask for a kind of place ──► choose a spot                     │

```

## One tick, in the order the code runs it

`Companion/Brain/CoordinateBrainTick.cs` owns this order. Every path, including the early ones, ends in the same finaliser.

1. **Observation.** `WorldObservation.Senses` refreshes the player, threats, whether a boss fight or world event is under way, projectiles, loot, light and the companion's own state, in that order. Light is refreshed here and is a field rather than a number: brightness at every open-air tile on a lattice through a window around the companion, and it is rebuilt early when the engine starts, stops or resizes the area it computes, because answering from a reading of a differently-sized frame is answering about a world nobody measured. Home protection and tool cooldowns update. Not every query recomputes every tick: route floods and opportunity searches keep bounded work across ticks and refresh on their own cadences, and the reach flood is refreshed by step 8 rather than here.
2. **Danger timing.** The arsenal estimates how soon the companion could intervene against the most urgent threat to you, and observation turns that into protection urgency: how late help would arrive compared with the threat.
3. **Time apart.** Companionship evidence — how long the two have been out of comfortable range — is recorded before any branch can skip the rest of the tick, so an interruption never resets it.
4. **Distant recovery**, if already under way, keeps flying the companion back through terrain and ends the tick there.
5. **Reflexes and shared safety.** Reflexes predict imminent collisions with enemy bodies and projectiles and hand the prediction to movement; they take nothing. Shared safety may then take the body for environmental escape. When it does, the ordinary activity is suspended, not failed, and the tick goes straight to the finaliser.
6. **Choosing.** The chooser prepares the seven behaviours, values them, lets each family nominate, picks the strongest nomination, checks it is still valid, and hands it to the activity owner, which keeps its identity and attempt record.
7. **Acting.** The chosen behaviour performs any immediate interaction and returns a position request. A reunion request from far enough away, with no tool in hand, may start distant recovery instead.
8. **Placing and moving.** Position selection resolves the request to a hover point; movement plans a route and steers along it, or hovers where it is. The resulting controls are then run forward against the reflexes' prediction and against any water or lava the body is not immune to, and bent away only if they would meet one, the activity keeping its attempt and its hands. Which liquids the body may enter is written once at the top of the tick from its immunities, and every search that tick reads it. Resolving is also what advances the reach flood, which is bounded per advance and grows across successive resolves rather than on the tick, so consumers asking between resolves read the flood the last resolve left. When nothing inside a follow objective can be accepted, the request aims at its own anchor until the objective's own arrival test is met, and no candidate is invented to stand in for one.
9. **Finalising.** One control grant applies the movement through the motor and decides what the hand may do; the independent arsenal acts if the hand is available; progress and outcomes are observed and credited to the executing attempt; and the HUD snapshot is published.

Being downed bypasses steps 2–8: the NPC lifecycle holds the body and revokes the hand through the same finaliser.

## Three families, seven behaviours

A **family** is a purpose. A **behaviour** is one concrete way of serving it, with a real target. A family never runs on its own; it only ever puts forward the best behaviour it has.

| Family | Behaviour | What it is trying to accomplish |
|---|---|---|
| Gathering | **Mining** | Find ore the current pickaxe can break, retain the vein, hover where the ore is actually in tool reach, and break it. |
| Gathering | **Chopping** | Find a tree it may cut, prefer one the player is not already cutting, reach it and fell it with the axe. |
| Combat | **Hunting** | Put the body where a shot can exist. It does not choose who gets shot or which weapon fires — that is the hands, in every activity. It never flies toward an enemy no weapon could hit from anywhere it can reach. |
| Combat | **Guarding** | Put itself where it can remove harm that is coming for you, in time to matter. A fight too long to win is worth less guarding, so a boss's life bar does not outrank the small enemies on you. |
| Nearby assistance | **Lighting** | Take a region of measured dark air it can reach, and work supplied torches into it from where it hovers, rather than visiting one site and leaving. |
| Nearby assistance | **Collecting** | Fetch a drop the bag or your inventory can take, or break a pot for contents that are unknown until they fall. |
| Nearby assistance | **Keeping company** | Rejoin you when separation matters; otherwise hover beside you, drifting slowly around where it settled. |

### How the offers are compared

Each behaviour **prepares** first: it searches for its opportunity, checks policy and reach, and captures a value, a target and how long the job would take. Comparison only reads what preparation captured, so comparing cannot quietly retarget or advance a job. Preparation is scheduled within a per-family time allowance; a behaviour that did not get to prepare this tick is recorded as deferred, not as having nothing to do.

Every offer also says whether it is **usable**, **unresolved** (worth approaching while its feasibility is still being worked out), **known unusable**, **forbidden by a setting** or simply **absent**. A positive value is never allowed to win beside an absent, forbidden or unusable offer.

The shared evaluator then multiplies each raw value by the same factors, so any zero vetoes an offer:

- **protection** — for optional excursions only, how much your need for help discounts going elsewhere;
- **commitment** — the running behaviour keeps a small bonus so near-ties do not flip back and forth;
- **horizon** — work that would outlast the time before a threat arrives is charged for the overrun;
- **reunion** — for excursions, the cost of the delay the job adds to getting back to you, which grows when you are leaving and when the way back is long.

Keeping company is not an excursion, so none of the excursion charges apply to it; it is modestly discounted instead while useful work is available within the activity allowance, so work can win without the companion wandering off. A hunt that already has the shot, or whose remaining flight is short, is treated the same way: nearby fighting is ordinary work rather than leaving.

Each family nominates its highest final value. The strongest nomination wins. Before it starts, its captured target is checked again (gone, replaced or re-typed targets are refused), and hunting and guarding must get a firing position admitted by position selection. A refused winner keeps its raw value on the record with a named reason, and the next prepared offer is considered without searching again.

## What each behaviour does

**Mining** keeps one vein at a time. It only offers ore the pickaxe in its slot can damage, and it reaches ore wherever free space touches it, ceiling ore included, because a body that hovers has no standing pose to prefer: one query ranks every free cell within reach of the tile and asks the reach sense about each, nearest first. Before every swing it checks, from where the body actually is, that the ore is in the player-derived reach and has an exposed face. Its value includes the approach and the tool time to the next block breaking, from native damage. It never digs ordinary terrain to make a way in. A flight toward ore whose approach is still unknown is given up if the body is not covering ground, and that same tile is not offered again until the terrain changes. A vein cleared by the player is not counted as its work, and a cleared tracked portion of a vein that continues is partial, not complete.

**Chopping** works the same way for trees: nearest admitted trunk it can reach, the player's own tree only when nothing else is left, and a trunk the player takes is released by name. Home protection and the chopping setting are checked again at the swing.

**Hunting** does not shoot, and it does not choose who gets shot. The arsenal is the only chooser of weapon–target pairs: useful damage, finishing, piercing several bodies in one line, threat removed. Those are one question because a piercing weapon through three targets can beat a harder hit on one, and a weapon that goes through walls does not need the body to move at all. The gun runs in every activity — mining, lighting, walking, hunting — whenever the hand is free. Hunt's job is to put the body where a shot can exist. It may fly toward a creature as a landmark; from the new spot the gun is free to fire at someone else. It starts moving once it knows a hit is possible from somewhere, without freezing the standing tile first, because the thing it is lining up will have moved. It does not fly toward something no weapon could hit from anywhere it can reach. If a trajectory exists from the current pose and a weapon can take it and the gun does not fire, that is the arsenal, not hunt.

What is built today still picks a pursuit NPC and asks position selection for a `LineOfFire` stand, admitted only with `FromHere` or `AfterMoving`. Existence is the arsenal's `ForecastAttack`, not a straight ray: a handful of nearest stands are solved per target, and a solvable stand the flood has not yet claimed is already AfterMoving, so hunt can start moving without freezing the spot. `Unknown` is an unfinished flood with no arc, and it still scores zero. A stationary hunt that produces no movement and no relevant attacks is deferred for a while. Its value yields as the companion's own danger rises, except a shot it already has from here. What the body is flying toward, what the hands are aiming at and what a shot actually struck are recorded as three separate things. It never crosses water or lava to reach an enemy, because those are walls to every search until an immunity opens them.

**Guarding** binds its offer to one specific enemy generation and the harm it can prevent, asks for a position within a wide band that can hit what threatens you, and stays committed through brief dips in pressure. It is refused when no useful intervention position exists, without being forgotten. Its value is the pressure on you times a usefulness share: full while flying to a place with a shot and then removing the threat fits inside a useful length of fight, and falling in proportion beyond it, which is what lets small enemies on you keep full protection while a boss does not. A threat it has proven it cannot shoot from anywhere it can reach is worth no guarding at all, so it goes about other things and leaves avoiding that threat to the bend every movement gets; while it has not yet worked out whether such a place exists, it values the threat by the fight alone. A threat no weapon can damage keeps its full value, because staying out of its way is that same bend rather than a job of its own.

**Lighting** works a dark region rather than visiting a site, which is the difference between lighting a cave and trickling one torch into it per search. The light field nominates the regions of dark air nearest the companion's own body — not the player's, and not a disc around it — and a region whose middle lies outside the work radius around the player is somebody else's darkness and is passed over. Every remaining region contributes its tiles, because offering only the nearest would let one sealed pocket hide every reachable region behind it, and dark air under a floor is nearer than a shelf across the room. The executor takes the first site whose access it can prove, scanning outward from the body, and the game's own Smart Cursor still owns attachment and spacing.

Whether a site wants a torch is its whole neighbourhood read from the engine at its own finer stride, never off the field's lattice, whose stride is wider than the lit patches this has to resolve; a neighbourhood nobody has read is refused alongside a lit one, because a torch spent on an unmeasured place is spent on a guess. A light the companion is carrying counts as darkness here, which is the exact opposite of how the held torch reads the same samples and is right for the same reason: a permanent torch exists so the place stays lit after the carried one walks away, and a companion that refused every site inside its own glow could never light the cave it was standing in.

The reach sense answers first, and a route search runs only for the tile already chosen. A site the companion must fly to needs a hover beside it that the flood holds, and a site whose every hover lies behind water or lava is refused by name rather than reported as no candidate; a site in a ceiling or across a chasm is the same query as any other, because nothing about height is special to a body that hovers.

The search asks the reach sense, never a route search of its own: each candidate site is one lookup in the flood the sense already ran, nearest first, and a route is planned only for the site it chooses, through the same movement request every other destination uses. That is what removed the starvation the 14 September 09:28 capture showed, where a fixed count of per-site route searches answered Unknown, remembered nothing, and re-asked the same nearest sites every rescore; the count and its tunable are gone. What bounds the scan now is the tick's planning deadline, and a scan the deadline cuts before it finds a site reports itself unresolved and resumes at the next rescore rather than waiting the full cadence or reporting the deadline as a fact about the world. A refusal proven by a complete flood is remembered so the next scan advances past it; a not-yet-known answer from a flood still growing is neither charged nor remembered. The record carries which sites each scan asked and what the sense answered, so the next capture can say what was considered. The boundary script fails any activity or interaction that calls a route query directly, so the rule holds for jobs not yet written.

Its value is a base plus a term for how much dark the region holds, under a ceiling that keeps the biggest cavern from outbidding protecting you, raised by a factor when you are carrying no light of your own. Enablement is the torch setting plus a torch in supply. The job survives a placement, so a region needing several torches is worked through from where the companion then stands.

**Collecting** compares a known drop against a pot's possible contents. A known drop has to lie somewhere the flood holds, is valued by the amount that will actually fit, is proved again if it moves, and its attempt is concluded from what the bag actually received. A drop still falling is offered where it will come to rest rather than where it is, forecast with the game's own item motion, including the slower fall and the lagged velocity the game applies inside water, honey and shimmer; a drop whose fall outlasts the forecast's horizon is undecided rather than guessed at. Contact pickup still happens during any behaviour.

**Keeping company** chooses, inside one activity, between rejoining you and relaxing nearby. Rejoining is priced against your intent region, the box the player-intent sense carries ahead of your feet: the pull is continuous from the region's own centre outward, never exactly zero while you are travelling, and it joins the far slope at the region's edge without a step, so a moving player is itself a reason to move and the body no longer coasts to whichever edge it entered by. That far pull never sits at 1, so a proven job further across the screen can still win; lost sight and return pressure raise it; the hard leash at fly-home distance is not that cap, and that one still drops everything. Being with you is not geometry alone: arrival needs the body at rest inside the region for a whole rescore, because two bodies passing each other are momentarily a few pixels apart and neither has arrived anywhere. Changing method is a change of request kind, and a change of request kind cancels whatever the body is doing, so inside the region the other method has to be wanted for a rescore and asked for from rest; outside it, or hovering in tiles you are asking for, the change is immediate. Relaxing nearby is hovering: the body drifts slowly around the place it settled, a little up, down and to either side, and never hangs perfectly still. There used to be a stroll to a random safe cell with a rest between strolls, and it went on 15 September 2026, because a stop between every flight was the stop-start motion the first orb play showed; a hold now also keeps the reach flood growing, which the stroll had only ever done by accident. It gives way when you need the space: aim a block at the tiles it is hovering in, or walk into a one-tile-tall passage it is hovering in, and it moves back to your side, while aiming a weapon or torch at it changes nothing. It never claims a finished job: its attempts are recorded as executed.

## Shared systems

These are not behaviours and never compete in the comparison. Every behaviour runs through them, and each one's mechanism, at the depth a change needs, lives in the CLAUDE.md of the folder that owns it.

- **Observation.** One account of the world per tick, so no two behaviours derive the same fact differently.
- **Light, as a field.** Brightness at open-air tiles across a window around the companion — packed dirt, platforms and furniture are not darkness — taken only where the game's lighting engine has actually computed light, asked of the engine's own bounds rather than approximated by the screen rectangle. A tile the engine has not computed is unknown and never dark, because spending a torch on a zero nobody measured is spending one on a guess. Every light that is only in the world while something carries it is discounted, read from the engine's own per-frame list of them rather than from a list of ours, so a held torch, a light pet, a glowstick, a mining helmet and the companion's own torch arrive by one door and none of them is enumerated anywhere in the brain. The engine merges and propagates light by taking the greater of two values and never by adding, so a reading brighter than any carried light could produce is the world's own light exactly and is kept whole, while a reading a carried light could account for is bounded and not narrowed further. The two consumers read that bounded case in opposite directions and both are right: deciding whether to hold a light it is unknown and is skipped, because the light in hand is the reason the tile looks bright; deciding whether to leave one behind it counts as dark, because what stays after the carrier walks off is at most what he was carrying. Legacy lighting fills no such list, so under it a carried light reads as room light.
- **Reach, as a sense.** Where the body can fly to, flooded over the free space around it from where it is and read by position selection, tool access and lighting alike rather than owned privately by whichever of them asked first. Free space is the set of tile corners the body's circle fits at, each joined to its neighbours; water and lava are walls to the flood until a mastery immunity opens them, and honey and shimmer are not. There is one flood and not two, because every edge of that graph runs both ways and a place the body can fly to is a place it can fly back from, so the walker's returnable region and its round-trip proofs have no subject here. At spawn the region is empty rather than stale, and what starts the first flood is the first query that asks the sense. A terrain edit empties it again only where the flood actually looked: every retained search records the box it explored, an announced edit is tested against that box, and an edit the search never read leaves it valid. The answer is reachable, not yet known, or proven absent, and only an absence resting on a flood that ran out of region before its budget is a refusal worth remembering; an unfinished flood is unknown, so an activity that declines it is declining to start on an unanswered search rather than asserting the place does not exist. The flood is a disc rather than a world: bounded at twice a known radius of straight-line distance around the body, so in an open world it finishes instead of running to the search's node limit and finishing unexhausted, which is what the first orb tree did and which left nothing provably unreachable for the life of a session. Inside the known radius a tile the finished flood never claimed is proven absent, meaning every route to it is a detour of about three times its straight line; beyond it the answer is not yet, never absent, because the flood was not asked to go there. Once the body has travelled half that radius from the root a replacement flood grows there while the old one keeps answering, so ordinary travel never empties the region. Underground the ball is mostly rock and closes in a few resolves; in open sky it is the whole disc and takes a few hundred ticks.
- **Player intent.** Your recent movement is kept as a short, bounded history. Steady travel in one direction builds confidence that you are going somewhere; crossing the same small area, stopping, or using tools lowers it; a brief turn weakens the old direction and sustained backtracking replaces it. It estimates a journey, never a destination. That journey becomes a place: the intent region is a box carried ahead of your feet by your own observed pace, low-passed so a halt does not snap it back onto you, grown a little while you travel, and clamped to your own half-screen so the companion is somewhere you can see. It has a gradient, zero at its centre and rising through its edge with no step, measured on both axes rather than as a radius because a tile on the next floor down is not being with you. Every "how far from the player" question in the brain measures to it — the reunion pull, the work radius around you for ore and trees, the lighting search centre, the meeting place's fallback anchor, the reach the chooser allows an excursion — so work near you is work near where you are going rather than behind you.
- **Danger to each actor.** Danger to you and danger to the companion are measured separately (see below).
- **Companionship cost.** Time apart accumulates from observation, and the reunion charge combines how fast you are leaving with how long the companion's route back would take.
- **Shared safety.** Environmental escape, the one response that takes the body away from its job. It can act with no ordinary offer at all. Avoiding a hit is not in it: movement bends whatever the body is doing away from a predicted hit and leaves the job its attempt and its hands, which replaced a collision reflex and a combat-spacing search that each suspended the job — the second of them is what parked the orb beside a zombie in the first play.
- **Position selection.** Turns a kind of place into a hover point, scoring cheap factors first and paying for trajectory checks only on a shortlist. The candidates are the free corners near the request's anchor, under a ceiling a few tiles above your feet so the companion is beside you rather than over your head, and each is the same corner the search uses for its nodes, so every candidate is somewhere the body fits. Reachability is a tier rather than a factor: while any candidate sits inside the flooded region only those are scored, because a spot the body cannot reach is not a worse spot but no spot. When a follow request accepts nothing, it answers nothing, and movement aims at the request's own anchor until the request's own arrival test is met; the walker's substitute, the reachable tile that most closed the gap, was a destination that could be arrived at without achieving anything, and it is gone. A destination already held is kept, without a search, for as long as it still belongs to the region it was admitted against: a follow spot inside the intent region and not in your way, a firing stand whose arc still solves against a target that has not moved past a hold slack, a partial-progress tile the body has not reached that still closes the gap; retention is a rule about membership, not a bonus a rival has to outscore. A firing stand is proved against the target's forecast at the body's estimated arrival and then across a window as long as the trip itself, capped so a flight across the world is not asked for an arc that holds indefinitely, because the shot was being proved against a slime that would not be there, and a stand whose shot closes before the trip ends was never a reason to go. Every stand a real solve refused is remembered against this terrain and this target generation standing about here, and which refusals survive the body moving depends on what each was about: a stand with no arc is a fact about the line to the target and holds from anywhere, while a stand refused because the shot would close before the body could fly there is a fact about that trip and holds only from where the trip started. Whether the sweep has finished is a separate question keyed on the stands and the target alone, never on the body, because keying it on the body was what stopped a moving companion's search from ever finishing. When the memory fills, the target that has sat in it longest loses its entries rather than everyone losing theirs, so one target's refusals arriving cannot restart every other sweep in progress. A shortlist therefore advances past refusals instead of restarting from the top; a search that ran out of solves or time before it found a stand says so rather than reporting that nothing exists.
- **Movement.** One request surface, one game-free core, one motor. The body is a twenty-pixel circle that flies: no gravity, no ground, no jump, no breath. The engine's own tile collision is switched off for it, and the mod's own contact keeps it out of walls — the circle against the tiles, pushed out along the nearest face, its speed into the wall killed and its slide along the wall kept — run in the motor after the tick's velocity is chosen, and run identically by every headless tool from the same source, so there is one body and no second one to keep in agreement with it. Slopes are full tiles to it, platforms are passable, a closed door is solid until the door interaction opens it, and water and lava hurt on touch, which is why they are walls to every search until an immunity opens them. A destination is planned as a route over the free-space graph, corner to corner, priced by length and by how tight the corridor is so a route prefers the middle of a passage, then smoothed by line of sight without losing the clearance the search paid for; the body follows it with steering that carries momentum, capped in speed, turning under one cap and gathering or shedding speed under a slower one, and easing into its arrival, where it does not stop but hovers around the place it arrived at. Any route with clearance is followable by construction, so there is no proof step between planning and moving and no class of route the planner proved and the body could not perform, which was the walker's whole failure. A search too large for one tick keeps its frontier, and while it is unfinished the body follows what it had, flies straight at the goal where the line is clear, and otherwise hovers where it is; a search that exhausts the free space is a proven absence, reported apart from unfinished. A retained search is thrown away by a terrain edit only where it looked, because the game announces every edit, including a door anybody opens or closes, and a search compares them against the box it explored. A spot the body keeps failing to reach is set aside for a while after two failed tries. Whoever ends a route says so, and the attempt is scored as completed, cancelled, pre-empted or failed rather than folded together. A dodge is neither a route nor a request: after the tick's controls are chosen they are run forward through the same contact against the predicted collision and against any water or lava the body is not immune to, and only if they would meet one are they bent to the heading that stays clear and keeps doing the job, a stop winning only when it is clearly safer than moving. Every place the body hovers around dies with the request that made it, and is replaced by where the body is whenever the body has no clear line to it, so a hover never pins the body against a wall. Nothing plans as if the body stood, and no performer derives a move of its own.
- **Tool access and world permission.** One reach query for mining, chopping, pots and torches, answered from the reach sense and never from a search of its own: the body's own position first, then every free cell within reach of the tile, nearest first. The set of world edits is closed — ore, trees, pots, torches, doors — and bed-anchored homes veto autonomous edits. Tiles are struck through the game's own pickaxe and axe routines with the pickaxe and axe in the companion's own slots, via a temporary player stand-in — an empty slot is no tool, so the work is offered as unusable rather than done with a tool never handed over — and a torch is placed through the game's Smart Cursor choice and spent only once it is placed. Whether a torch is *held* is a separate decision over the same light field, asked twice — of the air around the body, and of the air at your predicted feet — and it is deliberately asymmetric: either query reading measured dark air raises the torch, and only both reading measured and bright lowers it, under a minimum hold. So a companion crossing a lit floor toward a dark hole ahead has the torch up before it arrives, one it carries into an area the engine has not computed simply stays lit, and the hand does not flicker.
- **Continuity and progress.** The activity owner keeps identity, phase and attempt records, separates suspension from failure, and credits only observed native effects.
- **One control grant.** Movement and the hand are granted together, once, whoever proposed them: an ordinary behaviour, shared safety, recovery or the downed lifecycle.
- **Independent weapons.** The companion has no weapon of its own: it fires or swings whatever real item sits in either of its two weapon slots, from the game or any mod, read for its numbers and never run. A projectile weapon spawns its projectile, owned by you so kills and drops stay yours, composed with the class's free default ammo by the game's own rules; a swung weapon sweeps a sector around the body and strikes every hostile in it through the game's own strike path. An item whose behaviour lives outside its numbers is refused by the slot, with the reason shown. A magic weapon draws on a mana pool that mirrors your maximum and tires rather than refuses: an empty pool still casts, at about half strength, and recovers. The arc each projectile type flies is learned from the companion's own shots, with the game's arrow and thrown-object gravity as the starting guess, so a modded projectile misses once and then does not. The arsenal is the only chooser of weapon–target pairs: predicted useful damage, finishing, piercing several bodies in one line, harm removed, then a real trajectory. Weapon and victim are one choice, so a piercing shot through three targets can beat a harder hit on one, and a weapon that goes through walls does not need hunt to move the body. The gun runs in every activity whenever the hand is free. Hunt must not name the NPC the hands fire at. If a trajectory exists from the current pose and nothing is fired, that is this chooser, not hunt.
- **Incidental interactions.** After the hands have acted, on an ordinary tick with a free, idle hand, the companion breaks a permitted pot or places a torch in measured darkness if one is already within reach of where it stands. It never moves for it, never takes the current activity's own target, and credits the effect to no activity, so passing a pot on the way to light a cave breaks it without a detour or a second claim of work. It is skipped on a tick whose planning time is already spent.
- **Lifecycle and recovery.** Downing and revival belong to the NPC; distant recovery phases through terrain back toward you continuously, the one exception to the body's contact, and never teleports.
- **Experience.** The companion earns experience only from what it completes itself beside you — a fight finished, ore broken, a torch placed — credited from the attempt record with its attribution: a family's worth per productive effect for its own completion, half that when the work was shared with you, and nothing from your own actions. The level curve and the fraction toward the next level are what the notch draws; the mastery wheel will spend what a level grants once its contents are numbered.
- **Diagnostics.** The inspector, the per-tick recording, the sparse event stream and SessionReport read the retained evidence; none of them makes a decision. Schema 0.34.0 is the orb's: the walker's ground, press, descent, divergence, breath and predicted-body columns are gone, and the body row carries its wall contact and normal, the liquid it touches, its clearance, its desired velocity and the route's lookahead instead, so a reader that names its columns tells a walker capture from an orb capture by the schema alone and reads an older one as reduced coverage rather than as a misread column; 0.33.0 added the terrain revision, the intent region and its pull per row, and a preamble naming the world and the body's capabilities, so a capture can be replayed through a saved world and scored against what the body could do; 0.31.0 replaced the single ambient brightness number with the two field queries the torch decision actually reads and the reason it gave, names the dark region lighting nominated, counts the carried lights discounted that tick, reports both reach floods with a completeness flag beside them so an unfinished flood and a small one stop reading alike, and carries the travel rates as running values near the end of the row while route episodes and their stops land in the event stream. A journey is measured against the ticks the body could actually travel — downed ticks are subtracted, because a downed body is not travelling badly, it is not travelling — and every stop on a route carries the reason its producer attributed to it as it happened. Four overlay layers say what the companion means to do rather than what it sees: where following would be content, what the senses hold about the world the body has to deal with, what shared safety is responding to, and a strip of what the thinking cost, one column per tick against a fixed half-frame hairline so a spike reads as a spike. The offline replay prints planning time per route rather than only per run, because a run total cannot say whether a change spread its cost or built one expensive search. Each row also carries the combat identities, the admitted success region, and what writing the row before it cost, because a row cannot contain the time spent writing itself. Tool strikes name the activity attempt they were struck under; collection claims the yield it received; SessionReport joins those by identity and reports a claimed transfer no pickup delivered. A method that failed more than once with no credited effect is a finding; a hunt already refused as no reachable firing position is not reported as the hands refusing to shoot. Old playtest files are still schema 0.10.0, so the new joins skip on them rather than inventing a clean run. Every instrument reports one row per named case into a committed ledger, and a run is scored against the nearest clean ancestor run rather than read as one exit code: a case that stopped reporting is printed apart from one that passed, a pass rate carries its interval, a measure carries a direction and is never graded against a threshold nobody declared, and a recorded play becomes a run of play measures under the revision that wrote it, so two captures compare two builds rather than two afternoons. A red row tagged as a known limitation is carried and printed without failing the run. The scenario corpus can be mirrored, reduced to the smallest failing case, and cut out of a recording. The world run plays the whole brain and the native body through a real saved world on the engine's own tick, with the player moved along a recording's own track, and scores each place the player stood by whether the companion got there, called it unreachable, or had not finished flooding, excusing by name a place under water or lava the orb has no immunity for; it runs the same slice twice and files a red when the two passes disagree, and it is the instrument that found the companion's own digging never reaching its planner headless, because the engine's tile hooks were unwired in every other harness.

**Two examples of the shared parts meeting a behaviour.** A projectile arrives while the companion is mining: reflexes predict the hit and movement bends the tick's flight off the shot's line; mining is not suspended, keeps its vein, its attempt and its hand, and swings again as soon as the body is back in reach, because avoiding the hit rode on top of the job rather than replacing it. And a guard far from you: guarding only wins if protection urgency says the threat will reach you before help could otherwise arrive and position selection admits a spot with a real shot; moving closer to you is not itself the goal, and once the threat is gone the commitment is released.

## A route, a usable position and a productive effect are three different facts

- **A route** says the body can get from here to a tile. Finding one is not arriving, and arriving is not being useful.
- **A usable position** is a pose from which the behaviour's purpose is possible: the ore is inside real tool reach with an exposed face, the firing position has a solved trajectory, you are within comfortable range and in sight. Each behaviour judges this against the body's actual pose, not the waypoint it was sent to.
- **A productive effect** is what the game observed: the block's damage went up or it disappeared, the torch was placed, the item reached the bag, the enemy took damage. Swinging, arriving and being labelled "mining" prove none of these.

The recording keeps all three apart, so a reader can tell a companion that could not find a way from one that arrived and could not reach, and from one that reached and changed nothing.

## Moving with your journey, even by a different route

When you are travelling, keeping company does not chase a point projected from your velocity. It takes places along your apparent journey and prices each one by the companion's own routes: arriving before you costs the wait, arriving after you have passed costs the chase, and far guesses cost more because the evidence for them is weaker. So a route that leads on to your path ahead is taken forward to meet you there, while one that ends at a wall of rock is abandoned for the way round behind. The price is decided only once the companion's route flood has finished, because unfinished route times are upper bounds; until then it aims a short way ahead of you rather than at where you were. A place stays chosen until another is clearly cheaper, and is dropped when you stop, so the companion never keeps flying to a meeting point you have already left. It does not know where you are going, only the journey your recent movement describes. Until a place is priced, reunion aims at the leading edge of your intent region, the point on the region's boundary in your direction of travel, and a still player has no leading edge, so it aims at you. A priced place is a preference among the tiles the region accepts and never a second acceptance box: it used to widen the region by a growth of its own, so a far-off meeting place enlarged the very arrival test it was aiming inside and the body could satisfy following by standing anywhere along the line to a place it had not reached.

## Self-preservation is not player protection

The brain measures two dangers and never lets one stand in for the other.

- **Danger to the companion** comes from threats that can reach it, from the water or lava it is touching, and from recent damage, weighed against its remaining life. A threat's weight is the hit it would actually land after the game's own defence arithmetic, set against the life the body has left, so armour lowers it, wounds raise it, and the same enemy can weigh differently on you and on the companion. It drives shared safety's escape and makes hunting back off. A walking enemy counts as able to reach the hovering orb only when the orb is inside the walker's own height plus its highest jump, so a zombie under an orb high above it is no danger to the orb.
- **Danger to you** comes from threats that can reach you, and becomes **protection urgency** once it is compared with how soon the companion could actually intervene. It drives guarding, and it is the one place your danger reaches optional work — once, through the protection factor.
- **A boss fight or a world event** is a third observation, read from the game's own facts: a boss on screen, the Old One's Army, a blood moon, eclipse, pumpkin or frost moon while you are on the surface, an invasion while its invaders are still arriving and you are near the surface within its reach, or a lunar pillar's zone, which are the places the game runs them. An event nothing names is inferred the way the game itself decides the world is full: enemies that can reach either of you are weighed by the spawn slots the game counts them as (a worm is its head, not its eight segments), and only a weight held above the highest spawn cap the game has applied since the oldest of those enemies arrived counts, because ordinary spawning can never push a crowd past that. The highest cap, not today's, is the comparison because your own choices lower the cap under a crowd that has not moved: a calming potion or a peace candle thins future spawns and is not an invasion. While it lasts, optional non-combat work stops wherever the event reaches you, charged beside your danger rather than on top of it, and ordinary work returns the moment it ends.

A threat to you alone never makes the companion retreat; a threat to the companion alone never makes it guard you. Distance is not a stand-in for either: an enemy sealed in a pit may be harmless to both, and one far away may still arrive first.

## The health notch

The notch at the top of the screen shows the companion's family icon on the left, its name, level and health in the middle with mana and experience as two half-width bars beneath, and its behaviour icon on the right; hovering mana says how hard a spell would land right now, and hovering experience says how far the next level is. Gathering is a basket, combat is crossed blades, nearby assistance is a four-point spark, and each of the seven behaviours has its own symbol and tooltip. The icons name the purpose, not the weapon, ore or movement method. Both read one snapshot published at the end of each tick. While shared safety or recovery has the body, the paused behaviour stays visible but dimmed and its tooltip adds "(paused)"; when the companion is downed or has nothing selected, both icons show neutral marks and the tooltip says so. A "Stuck" label appears below health when travel is making no progress and nothing has paused the behaviour, so an escape in progress never reads as stuck. Clicking an icon or the health opens the profile card, dragging moves the notch, and right-clicking docks it again.

## What the player can change

Per character: mining and chopping each Off, Mimic or Auto; voluntary hunting; pot breaking; torch placement; following distance (Close, Standard, Free). Inventory and the four gear slots live in the profile card: two weapons, a pickaxe and an axe, each taking any real item its mechanism can use and saying why it refuses one it cannot; there is no armour, accessory or ammo slot, and ammo is free. The mastery page is a wheel of four lanes — guarding, movement, survival, gathering — and a selectable preview with no stat or ability effects yet. The companion mirrors your maximum life, defence and mana and reads your tool reach; its tool power is the pickaxe and axe in its own slots. A change to any of those reaches mining, chopping, lighting and pot work at once, the next time each looks for work: a longer reach or a stronger tool can open ore, trees and sites that were out of reach a moment ago, and a shorter one withdraws them.

## What this section does not claim

- A green headless check establishes its fixture, not behaviour in every cave or with every mod.
- Mastery is a preview: the dash, the speed and acceleration ranks, and the water and lava immunities the wheel names are not built. The body carries the multipliers and the immunity flags they would set, and nothing sets them yet.
- A closed door is treated as a wall by route search: the companion opens a door that is the only way, but prefers a detour it can fly over a door it could open.
- The gear refuses a modded item that fires through its own code, and that refusal has never been exercised: no modded item exists headless, and no modded game has been played with this tree.
- Besides the one orb play, which predates the hover, the easing and the evade layer, the recorded route is the only evidence about the orb's following: twenty of twenty places reached on one walker recording, measured to the player's body within the follow comfort. It is a replay of his track through the saved world with no enemies and no play behind it, and nothing else here about following has a capture behind it.
- Boss and event conduct beyond stopping optional work is not built: the wider range with a strengthening pull back to you, and the survival-first stance, that Expected Behaviour describes.
- An escape treats a shot over its only exit as impassable rather than weighing the hit against the hurt it would save.
- Help that would arrive too late makes protection more urgent rather than making the trip worth less: only a fight too long to matter, or a threat nothing it can reach can shoot, lowers guarding's value.
- Under legacy lighting the engine keeps no list of carried lights, so the field discounts nothing there and a torch in your hand reads as room light.
- One capture in Current Behaviour was taken of this body, 08:30 on 15 September on 0.26.0, before it hovered, eased and bent its flight on top of its job, so it describes the machinery this section replaced rather than this section. Every other capture is of the walking companion retired on 14 September, and the newest of those, 13:27 that day on the packaged 0.24.0, is the player track the orb is measured against headlessly rather than an account of what the orb does; the sessions behind the table's Current column are older still. They do not establish every biome, kit or setting, chopping on the 0.22.41 runs is Mimic, and 11:06 is mostly wet.
- The world run, the corpus mirror and the ledger are instruments over the harness's own fixtures and over recorded play; a green scoreboard says every case reported what it reported last time, not that the companion is good company.

# Behaviour By Behaviour

This table is the source of truth for what the companion is for, and it is maintained rather than written once: a new responsibility we discover mid-development becomes a row here first, stripped of any language about how it might be built, and the Expected Behaviour section above narrates it afterwards.

There is deliberately no column scoring how close each row is or where the blame sits. Both were tried and removed. A percentage conflates how complete a system is with how far off the behaviour looks, and those come apart badly: decision commitment moved from 55.5 to 22.3 ticks per choice on a single constant with no structural change at all, so any number claiming a behaviour was a fifth of the way there was describing a value rather than a system and would have swung to nearly all of the way there on a one-line edit. Blame is worse, because these systems are not separable enough for it — the clearest case in this table is a companion that looks bad at avoiding damage while the evidence points at the collision reflex interrupting its escape, which belongs to a different row entirely. Where a judgement of that kind is actually supported, it belongs in the System cell as a named hypothesis with the check that would settle it, which can be argued with; a confidence label cannot be.

## Maintaining this table

**The three columns answer three different questions and the commonest way to damage this table is to answer one of them in another one's cell.** Expected says what should happen, Current says what was observed, System says what is built and why we think the two differ. Mixing them is not untidiness — it makes the table lie.

**Expected never mentions a mechanism.** The test is whether the sentence would still be true after the system underneath it is thrown away and rewritten differently. "It searches more widely when a route fails" names a design and goes stale the moment that design changes; "it reaches the player through terrain where the first leg goes the wrong way — dropping into water, crossing under a wall, opening the gap on purpose in order to close it" names a behaviour and survives any implementation. The second form is also the one that leaves the design open, which is the point: a row that names a mechanism has quietly made an architecture decision in a document that exists to hold the requirements.

**Current is observation only, with the session named.** No reasoning, no cause, no "which suggests". If the sentence contains the word because, it is in the wrong cell. Numbers come from a recording and carry which one, so a reader can go and check; a sentence sourced from watching the game rather than reading the log says so, because a narrated impression and a measured column are not the same kind of claim and this table should not disguise one as the other.

**System carries both what is built and every hypothesis about why the gap exists, and each hypothesis is marked as one.** Write the word unverified, then the reading, then the specific check that would confirm or kill it — and prefer a check the existing telemetry could answer, because those are cheap. Where a plausible cause has been ruled out, say so and give the number that ruled it out; that is often more useful than the surviving hypothesis, because it is what stops the same idea being tried again in a month.

**Current without System misleads by omission, and the enemy selection row is the worked example of how.** It once read that nothing was selected on two thirds of ticks and that shots fired accounted for under 1% — both true, and together they read as "it picks targets and does not fire", which is the opposite of what is happening. Firing is one tick and the reload is 28% of the session, so it shoots perfectly well when it has a target; and a target chosen with no shot available accounts for 175 ticks in a session, so it is not picking blocked targets either. The actual finding is that it selects nothing at all while something is in range, and none of that is visible from the observation alone. Any row whose Current column invites an obvious explanation needs that explanation stated and tested in System, or the row will be read as whatever the reader assumes.

**When a new behaviour comes up mid-development, it becomes a row here first.** Strip the system language out of however it was said, write Expected, and leave Current and System as honest blanks if nothing has been observed or built. A row with two empty cells is more useful than no row, because it records that we want the thing.

**When something contradicts an existing row, the contradiction is resolved here before any code is written.** Two rows wanting opposite things, or a new observation that makes an Expected cell wrong, is a decision about what the companion is — so the losing version is deleted rather than left standing beside its replacement, and the reasoning goes in the commit that removes it. A table holding two answers to one question stops being a specification.

**Rows split when one of them turns out to be two responsibilities.** Finding a route was carved out of Traversing terrain for exactly that reason: one was about choosing a sequence of moves and the other about performing a single move, they fail independently, and a single row could not say which was wrong. If a row's Current column needs the word "sometimes" in two unrelated senses, it is probably two rows.

**Row order carries no meaning.** It was once ascending by a score that no longer exists. Put a new row anywhere.

# Known Limitations Of This Assessment

The recordings establish what happened in particular captured builds; current source supplies possible mechanisms. Neither alone isolates a cause. The research keeps those evidence classes separate.

- **The version comparison is observational.** The commitment change coincides with shorter recorded action runs, and its shared-stall mechanism explains a plausible feedback loop. The sessions differ in duration and circumstances; reverting the formula is not a measured post-revert recovery. A matched comparison is still needed.
- **Detailed episode analysis covers four cave sessions.** The wider local inventory contains thirty TSV files, including two empty captures, but inventory coverage is not equivalent to analysing every run. These episodes do not establish behaviour across biomes, progression stages, weapon kits or mod combinations.
- **Reader findings have bounded evidence.** The hunt-range check requires fresh, well-formed range rejections for every recorded pair and reports a potential issue; that bounded shortlist cannot prove an impossible pursuit. Ordinary-stall inference admits known ordinary movement owners, excluding safety and recovery ownership. Downing remains a potential issue without assuming that a safe escape or attack was available. These predicates have parser regression tests, but do not identify the cause of every unproductive episode.
- **Terrain capture is local and asynchronous.** Newer terrain events include material and tile state as well as shape and liquid. They still do not provide a simultaneous, complete world snapshot or reconstruct uncaptured terrain and enemy scripts.
- **Old captures do not identify every input needed for exact replay.** Version metadata is useful but lacks a complete source, package, configuration and capability identity. The input hashes in research identify the recordings that were read; they do not manufacture the missing state.
- **The cost of observing remains unmeasured.** Brain phase timers omit some recording and integration work, and some phases include candidate discovery and movement queries. Recorded expensive ticks do not establish that utility arithmetic, path search or telemetry alone caused the visible stutter.
- **Existing memory and light observations are narrower than the expected behaviour.** The short player trail and directed traversal archive serve particular movement questions. They are not a general account of explored ground or historical player permission. A few current light readings are not a spatial lighting map. These are capability gaps rather than evidence that every relevant observation is absent.
- **Helpful behaviour still needs direct judgement.** Native fixtures, source reasoning and historical recordings cannot establish that exploration feels natural, that ordinary risk is well judged, or that progression and other mods remain compatible. The proposals specify those acceptance cases without claiming they have passed.

## Maintaining this section

Keep each limitation tied to the evidence it restricts. Remove or narrow it only when a recording, source correction or controlled comparison resolves that restriction. An unavailable observation remains unknown; it must not become a confident negative or a reason to infer a hidden mechanism.

# Potential Improvements

The [research library](research/CLAUDE.md) compares the expected behaviour with historical attempts, current source, recordings and external work. Of its [three ranked proposals](research/proposal/CLAUDE.md), Proposal 1 was selected and is implemented with native headless evidence; the [implementation ledger](<research/Evaluation and Observability/Proposal 1 Implementation Ledger.md>) says, for every failure case and responsibility, whether a fixture holds it, holds part of it, or what is left. None of it has been judged in live play, and the next gate is a recorded playtest. The other two proposals remain conditional alternatives.

Proposal 1 grouped the seven behaviours into three purpose families and repaired candidate validity, activity progress, control ownership and purpose-specific destinations; The System In Place above describes the result. It deliberately left several behaviours unbuilt, each with an owner in the ledger: chaining several jobs into one trip, which is Proposal 3's bounded planning; the boss and event stance beyond stopping optional work; a projectile dodge that jumps; weighing a hit against the breath an escape would save; doors as passable ground for route search; and every movement ability beyond a ground jump. The target design must not be read as a claim that those work.

The second path asks whether a stronger shared execution framework is needed beneath those families. The third adds bounded planning where an immediate decision demonstrably misses consequential future work. The arsenal's weapon and immediate-target algorithms remain in place for this restructuring. All three retain navigation as a separate investigation: transition representation, search strategy, return evidence and physical execution can need different changes. The [discussion record](<research/Decision Architecture/Purpose Families and Shared Companionship.md>) preserves why this direction was selected, what alternatives remain, and why a chosen family must carry a real activity rather than discover after winning that it has nothing useful to do.

Each roadmap begins by checking the observer and reproducing a failure while separating choice, destination and body execution. It then branches on the measured result. More elaborate planning is justified by a demonstrated need for foresight; an A* replacement is justified by evidence about search work on an adequate movement model. Neither is a remedy inferred from the appearance of a stalled companion alone.

## Maintaining this section

Keep the detailed mechanisms, alternatives, experiments and reversal conditions in `research/`, with this section providing the route into them. Accepted behaviour belongs in the story and the table without naming its implementation. A researched proposal remains provisional until discussion or experimental evidence changes its status.

## Building

Navigation retains unfinished search while executing useful movement, and successful traversals become bounded route memories in the world save. Following recognises horizontal and vertical separation, while completed route steps measure progress through detours. A distant companion following normally can fly continuously back through terrain; combat and work cannot start that recovery, and flight never becomes learned route experience. Short movement searches handle local clearance and underwater escape. Protection retains relevant threats and considers the time needed to remove them. Ore jobs retain their vein through interruptions, revalidate approaches, and never dig ordinary terrain to reach ore. These capabilities have headless regression coverage; difficult caves and comfortable behaviour still need playtesting.

From this folder, `sh Tools/verify.sh` compiles against the installed tModLoader and checks the movement boundary. With the game closed, `dotnet build` also packages the mod for the next launch. A fresh launch avoids the unresolved in-game reload hang.

The complete feature lives under `Companion/`, grouped into its brain, character body, weapons, inventory, player and enemy integration, map integration and HUD. Movement is shared by travel, work and combat through `Companion/Brain/Infrastructure/Movement`. The orb's contact with terrain is the mod's own circle-against-tiles test, run identically in the mod and in every headless tool; `Tools/EngineReplay` runs the brain and that contact over Terraria's own tiles without opening a window, and `Tools/NavReplay` exercises the game-free movement core.

The tModLoader mod settings separately control the brain inspector and local telemetry. Both are enabled by default in this development build. Bind **Brain inspector** under Controls → Mod Controls; its default is `[`, but an older saved binding must be changed there. The key opens a layer menu for enemy forecasts, projectile paths, route steps, aiming and movement alternatives, attention and decision scores. Route drawing is selected initially. Closing the menu dismisses only the panel — the layers you selected keep drawing over the world, so you can watch the brain without the menu covering it. Untick **Show world drawings** to hide them all. Your layer selections are saved with the character.

When telemetry is enabled before entering a world, the session records player and companion movement, decision scores, execution and rejection state, actual pickups, projectile launches and contacts, effective damage, and rolling terrain snapshots. Files remain local under the tModLoader save directory's `ModSources/AICompanion/Telemetry` folder; nothing uploads automatically. For a bug report, leave the world and send the files sharing that run's timestamp. Metadata includes game, loader and loaded-mod versions. Disabling recording closes the current files immediately; enabling begins on the next world entry.

After leaving the world, `dotnet run --project Tools/SessionReport -- --timeline Telemetry` prints the chronological record followed by diagnostic findings. Narration is optional. The reader reports capture gaps and distinguishes observed events from inferred hesitation or causes; locally sampled terrain and recorded game state are not a video replay.

For several runs, use `dotnet run --project Tools/SessionReport -- --multirun Telemetry` or pass selected TSV paths. Add `--html /tmp/companion-playtests.html` in place of `--multirun` for a self-contained inspection page with a run selector, time scrubber, event filters, sampled state and causal event details. Rapid load attempts keep separate files, and metadata survives a zero-tick run. Unreadable captures remain explicit gaps. The page reports its sampling limits; full-source reports retain all recorded rows for diagnosis.
