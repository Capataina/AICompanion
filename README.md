# Multi... Player?: an AI companion that plays Terraria beside you

A singleplayer tModLoader mod. The companion is a small flying orb that travels with you, fights beside you with the weapons you hand it, drills the ore and trees you would, lights the dark and picks up what falls, all by its own choice and never on your orders.

## Overview: a competent friend playing the same game, at the same time, with its own opinions about what is worth doing

### What it is and is not: a presence beside you, never a pet, a minion, a second character or a unit you command

The companion is an NPC with its own closed set of abilities, a cargo bag and persistent character state. It has a pet's leash and a person's agency: it never leaves your side to go and do things elsewhere, and everything it does beside you is its own decision. It loosely accompanies you, fights nearby threats, collects useful drops, lights dark places and helps with nearby work. Standing idle beside you while a job is actually available nearby is a failure of the companion, not a rest state.

It is not:

- **a second player or a summon.** It is an NPC with its own reliable abilities. A weapon or tool you hand it is read for its numbers and used by the companion's own code, never run through the player's item-use system, so a weapon whose behaviour lives outside its numbers (yoyos, flails, spears, whips, summons, modded weapons with custom firing) is refused by the slot rather than faked.
- **an orders system.** There is no command queue, no "go here" click and no target painting. Toggles that say what kinds of work it may do are fine; telling it what to do next is not.
- **an independent agent.** It never goes off on an errand, guards a base while you explore, or fetches ore while you build. Distant work does not share your enemy-spawning context, and a companion somewhere you are not is broken.
- **a walker.** Its body is an orb that flies. Nothing in the mod plans a jump, a stair or a drop.
- **a machine-learning project.** Explicit game mechanics, consequence-priced decisions and free-space route search are the foundation, not reinforcement learning or a language model.

### Who it is for: Terraria players who want company that scales with the game

The player wants another presence sharing the adventure, without managing it: no orders, no inventory to curate, no loadout screen. It helps with what you are doing and never replaces doing it, so progression still comes from playing Terraria.

### Why it exists: the existing companion is an NPC that does some stuff

Games populated by AI that plays like people, and companions that play beside you as a friend would, are the aspiration. Terraria comes first because its modding loop rebuilds and reloads in game. The one established Terraria companion mod, TerraGuardians, reads end to end as an NPC that does some things; this companion is meant to be one that makes its own choices and scales with the game over time.

## Features: one orb, four slots, and everything it does beside you

### The body: a fist-sized orb that flies, drifts and never stands

- **It flies anywhere there is room for it**, passing every two-tile passage you pass and no one-tile crack, with momentum: it gathers speed, leans into turns and eases into arrivals, and never snaps or teleports during ordinary play.
- **Every liquid is air to it.** Water, honey, lava and shimmer never hurt, slow or transform it, from the start.
- **It is never still.** Once it arrives it drifts around the spot the way a hovering thing does.
- **It gets out of your way**: aim a block at the tiles it is hovering in, or walk into a one-tile passage it occupies, and it moves.
- **It is downed, never killed.** At zero health it drops; stand beside it for three seconds to revive it, or wait ten for it to get up on its own.
- **It flies home through terrain** when it falls far behind, continuously and visibly, the one time it ignores walls.

### Combat: it fights with the two weapons you hand it, choosing stand, weapon, target and aim together

- **Any real weapon, from the game or a mod**, whose use is aiming and releasing: a projectile weapon fires its own projectile (owned by you, so kills and drops stay yours), a swung weapon sweeps an arc around the orb.
- **One decision covers where to stand, which weapon, which target and where to aim**, so a piercing shot through three enemies can beat a harder hit on one, and a weapon that passes through walls needs no repositioning.
- **It learns from its own shots**: how each projectile type flies, and how each weapon lands on each kind of enemy, so a modded projectile misses once and then does not.
- **Ammo is free; magic tires rather than stops it**: an empty mana pool still casts, at about half strength, and recovers.
- **It protects you without being your bodyguard**: what is on you matters most, and a boss's life bar does not outrank the small enemies hitting you.

### Work: mining, chopping, lighting and collecting, near you and on its own initiative

- **Mining** with the pickaxe you hand it: ore it can reach and break, one vein at a time, never digging ordinary terrain to get there.
- **Chopping** with the axe you hand it, preferring trees you are not already cutting.
- **Lighting**: it finds measured dark air and places torches of its own where your own smart cursor would, none used up; a torch you hand it decides which kind.
- **Collecting** drops that fit in its bag or your inventory, including drops still falling, and breaking pots in passing.
- **Homes are protected**: rooms containing a bed are off limits to its edits.

### Progression: your stats, your gear and its own experience

- **It mirrors your maximum life, defence and mana**, so a life crystal for you is one for it.
- **It earns experience** from its own kills and work in full and from yours at half, on a curve that needs the same kills per level in any world.
- **A mastery tree** of stat, speed and immunity upgrades bought with points, laid out as a symmetric wheel of four lanes (Combat, Movement, Survival, Gathering). No node ever switches a behaviour on or off.

### The interface: a status notch and a profile card in the game's own look

- **The notch** above the companion shows health, mana and experience; drag it to move it, right-click to dock it, click to open the card.
- **The profile card** holds the companion's identity, its standing controls (mining and chopping each Off, Mimic or Auto; torch placement on or off), and pages for Inventory (two weapon slots, a pickaxe slot, an axe slot and the bag) and Mastery. Shift-click moves items between you and the companion.
- **While a companion is up, enemies spawn twice as often**: two of you clear a screen twice as fast, so the world sends twice as much.

## Behaviour: what the companion is held to

### Goals: the beliefs every behaviour answers to

- **The companion is with you or it is broken.** Everything it does happens within reach of you.
- **Its choices are its own.** It picks what to do from what is around it; you never issue orders.
- **Keeping you company is what it does only when there is nothing else worth doing**, never a job that competes with real work.
- **Strength comes from you, the tree and the gear you hand over**, never from gear it owns.
- **A mastery node never gates a behaviour.** Nodes change numbers, speed and immunities; a behaviour is available or it is not.
- **Weak beats boss-killing.** A companion that solos a boss removes you from the game; a slightly under-tuned one is a friend who needs you.
- **Progression still comes from playing Terraria.**

The fit test for any feature: would a good friend playing beside you do this on their own, without being told and without leaving your side, using what you handed them? If it needs an order, a trip, a fifth slot, or a body that stands on something, it is not this mod's.

### The target as a half-hour of play: a story written with no reference to how any of it is achieved

This section describes the companion we are building, as a player would experience it. It is written as one continuous half-hour of play, from spawn to the first boss. It makes no reference to how any of it is achieved, and no reference to what the mod currently does. It is the target.

One idea generates all of it. **The companion is a competent friend playing the same game you are, at the same time, with its own opinions about what is worth doing.** It is not a pet, not a follower and not a unit you command. It hangs around roughly where you are because that is where the game is happening. It never waits for your permission and it never copies you. Its body is a small flying orb, a machine the size of a large fist, so it goes where there is room for it and nowhere there is not, and it uses the gear you hand it.

#### 0:00: You load in

The world loads on a clear morning and the companion is already in the air a few tiles off, a little above head height, turned whatever way it last looked. It does not snap round to face you and it does not park itself over your shoulder; it takes in what is around it (a few trees on the slope, nothing dangerous, nothing dark, nothing on the ground worth picking up), and having found nothing better to do, it stays near and idles. Idling is not hanging still. It drifts a little, sways when you sway, turns as you turn. The space around you is a place it is allowed to be, not a point it must occupy. When you set off to the left it comes with you rather than behind you. The space around you goes with you, leaning a little ahead the way you are heading but never so far ahead that you are no longer in it, and the companion keeps moving through the whole of that space at roughly your own pace: easing forward to its leading edge, letting itself fall back toward the middle, rising over your head and dipping down again. It never aims for one spot in that space, and it is never the thing that flies to a place, stops there, lets you walk out from under it and then flies to catch up. That space is roomy, so it is not forever on top of you, and it opens out a little further the further it has leaned ahead of you; and most of it is above you rather than below, because a drone idles over your head, underground as much as on the surface, so when nothing is going on you mostly find it somewhere above you. It is much quicker than you are, so being level or ahead of you is the ordinary case, and being behind you is what happens when it stopped for something rather than a policy of trailing. It moves the way a thrown thing moves, gathering speed, leaning into a turn, easing off as it arrives; it never snaps from one place to another. Getting where it was going is not stopping there, either. Once it arrives it keeps drifting a little around the spot, up and down and to either side, the way a hovering thing never quite holds still, so at no point in the whole half-hour is it a thing parked in the air. When it sets off again it gathers speed over a moment and curves into the new direction rather than cutting to it in a straight line, and however quick it is at full pace (still twice your running speed, and quicker still when your boots are), it is never so quick off the mark that it looks like it jumped.

While you stay on the quiet surface it wanders around you, dips to look at something on the ground, rises again and sometimes just hangs there. A useful tree further away can be worth the trip when nothing nearby needs attention, and the pull back toward you grows with how far that trip would take it outside the space around you: none while it is still inside it, more as the gap opens, and even at its strongest it never outweighs a job genuinely worth doing, so being apart from you is not on its own a reason to drop real work; only the distance at which it would lose you altogether takes all of its attention. Keeping you company is what it does when there is nothing worth doing, not a job that competes with the others. Slimes trapped below a ledge do not change that by merely existing. A bat approaching you does: helping soon matters more than the same distant tree. If there is nothing useful further away either, it has no reason to disappear into an unexplored passage just to keep moving. And however far it ranges, it never climbs very far above you: a fist-sized thing thirty tiles up is a thing you cannot see, and it knows that, so it lives in the band of air a few tiles above your head and comes down to work. Even in a low tunnel it takes the middle of the air rather than the floor of it, and it keeps its distance from anything hostile the way it keeps its distance from the rock; it goes close to a wall to drill it or light it, and drifts back out into the open once that is done.

#### 2:00: The slime that matters and the one that doesn't

A blue slime appears at the left edge of the screen, bouncing away from you, and the companion does nothing about it at all. It has looked at that slime and worked through it honestly: the bow you gave it cannot reach from here, and the only thing that would put it in range is a long flight to the left that ends with the companion thirty tiles behind you and a slime dead that was never going to reach either of you. It is not worth doing, so it is not done, and the companion does not fidget about it or half-start toward it. It also does not back away from that slime. Eight hops from connecting is not something that is about to land; treating it as if it were would mean flying the wrong way for no reason.

Put a bat at that same edge, with a clear line and nothing in the way, and the answer changes without the distance changing. The bat will be here in a second or two. That is coming, so the companion shoots it while still travelling. Put a slow, heavy thing there instead (something that will take five seconds to arrive and an age to put down), and that is a different kind of problem again: it is not about to hit anyone this instant, but leaving it means living with it, so helping with it matters more than the hop-count suggests.

Put the bat and the slime both on a path toward you. The bat will be here in a second, the slime in five, and there is not time to put both down before something lands. What it is weighing then is which hit it would rather take, not which creature is more of a project to finish. A tap on the slime, then the bat gone, then the slime finished, is an ordinary way to spend those seconds once both are in reach, because the slime is not about to land and the bat is. It also minds which way each hit shoves what it hits. An arrow knocks a creature away from the side it came from, so the companion shoots from your side of the slime rather than from beyond it, and the slime is pushed away from you instead of into your face. It learns how hard each weapon it holds shoves each kind of creature by watching what its own hits actually do, so a heavy thing that barely moves and a light one that flies are handled differently without anyone telling it which is which. Standing behind the slime with a bow that shoves hard and a staff that barely does, it takes the staff's weaker shot rather than knocking the slime into you, and it slides round to your side, where the bow's harder hit pushes the slime away, because from there the bow is the better shot again. And its two weapons are a pair, not two separate tools: if one leaves a creature burning or slowed and the other hurts a burning or slowed creature more, it notices from its own hits and starts opening with the first and following with the second. How to use each weapon is learned the same way, from what its shots actually achieved rather than only from how they flew. Arrows that curve toward whatever they are fired near teach it that where it aims still decides how soon they connect, not that aim stops mattering; a shot that goes through one slime into the next teaches it to line slimes up; a shot that bursts into shards behind what it hits teaches it to hang where the shards carry on into the creatures beyond; a weapon that throws several things at once teaches it how wide that spread really lands. Which weapon, which creature, where to hang and how to aim are one choice to it, made for what gets done (creatures put down sooner, more of them hit, none of them shoved into you), however unusual the weapon you hand it. What it never does is treat the longer fight as a reason to ignore the thing that is about to connect.

What it is weighing, across all of that, is how the thing actually moves, how soon it can really get here, and how much of a fight it would be, not whether something living is on the screen.

A fight with more than one thing in it is a stretch of moves, not one choice of where to hang. Three zombies below and a Demon Eye beside it (the ordinary flying eye, not the boss): it rises, shoots the eye so the knock sends it down onto the zombies, then throws from where that line is, because that stretch puts more of them down than shooting the eye in place and then the zombies one by one. Hand it a bow and shurikens against a slime dropping onto you: the bow first, because that hit shoves the slime off, then the shurikens, because those hurt more once it is off you. The other order (shurikens until it is dead, or standing still until the perfect line exists) is the one it does not take. While it waits for a line it likes, it is still doing something: a worse shot that still connects, a pickup at its feet, a torch on the wall it is already next to. Hanging in the air doing nothing until the best moment arrives is not a fight.

The same stretch decides which of three creatures dies first. It is not always the most dangerous and it is not always the closest. An order that knocks one into the others, or that deletes the one about to land and then the rest, beats an order that treats each creature as its own project. Change the knock: a staff that barely shoves makes lining them up the wrong stretch, and it shoots to kill instead. Change who is about to land: the bat still dies first, and the lining-up waits.

Change one thing and the answer changes with it. Stop walking, turn to a tree and start swinging, and the same receding slime becomes worth killing, because now the flight costs nothing: you are not going anywhere, and the companion will be back at your side before you have finished the trunk. Change a different thing instead: leave the slime where it is but have it bouncing toward you rather than away, and it becomes worth killing immediately, because it is going to arrive whether anyone goes anywhere or not. The companion stops keeping you company and fights it: it picks where to be, which weapon and which shot together, and because it is so much faster than you, a fight this small never leaves you on your own for long. Fighting does not lock that slime as the shot: if three bats line up while it moves, the shot it actually takes is the one through the bats, because that is the better use of the weapon from where it now is, and where it goes next is chosen with that shot in mind rather than before it. A weapon that would go through the wall does not need anyone to move; it just shoots. Where a wall does cut its line, it rises a tile or two, or slips sideways, and shoots from the new angle without breaking stride; you do not break stride either. What it never does is put itself between you and something to soak a hit; it is a fighter hovering where it can shoot, not a shield. Although, after you start breaking a second tree, it looks around and notices that the nearest threat, another slime, is so far that it would realistically not reach you any time soon, and the companion decides that instead of a grand repositioning to kill a "not so urgent" threat, it joins you on the wood.

Hand it a sword instead of a bow and the same slime is a different question. A sword means going to the slime, and the companion knows what that costs: it swoops in, hits, and pulls up and away before the slime can bounce into it, the way a wasp works a picnic. A sword against a slime that is coming to you anyway is a good trade; a sword against a slime thirty tiles away is a worse trade than the bow's, because now the whole trip is the price rather than the arrow. With both a sword and a bow in its hands, it takes the bow shot when a bow shot exists and closes with the sword when the thing is already on top of one of you.

#### 4:00: Trees, and reading where you are going

You walk past a single oak and neither of you stops, because one tree is not worth breaking a journey for. Two minutes later you pass a stand of four grown together, and this time the companion peels off, sinks to the base of the nearest trunk, and a beam comes out of it and eats through the wood in a few seconds; the tree comes down, it sweeps up the wood and the acorns, and it comes back: eight seconds gone and twelve tiles to make up, which it makes up in two. The extra wood is worth the short separation in this scene. Put danger ahead of you, or have you hurry away, and the same trees can be left standing. Their number alone does not settle the decision.

Later you hurry through a long tunnel without stopping and it lets ordinary work pass. You are covering ground, and repeatedly peeling off for ore would leave it spending the journey catching up even at its speed. Slow down and move around one chamber placing torches, and the answer changes: you are still active, but you are staying in the area. It can help with nearby ore without waiting for you to hold a pickaxe or start the same task.

There can still be time for a small job while you keep walking. A little exposed ore lies beside the tunnel, and the companion can drift over, drill it, collect the drops and comfortably catch you before the next bend. It stops briefly to do so. If you accelerate or the drops fall somewhere awkward, it leaves the rest and comes with you. The same judgement follows it into a fight or a dark chamber: useful help is weighed against how long you will be apart and what getting back together will require, rather than treating every pause as wrong or every nearby opportunity as worth taking.

While that is happening the ground ahead splits into a high ledge and a low path, both running right, and you jump up to the ledge. The companion simply rises with you: there is air above the ledge and it goes there, on your right, a little up, as if the ledge were not there. Then you change your mind, turn, and drop into a ravine, and the companion comes down with you straight away, a little ahead of you, because it read the turn and the drop as one movement, but it does not race to the bottom of the screen, because the space it moves in leans down with you and never so far that you have left it. You think better of it and climb back out, and it is out before you are.

Say it had been shooting a zombie on the surface when you dropped into that ravine. The zombie was worth something a moment ago, with you standing beside it; with you going down, killing it would mean hanging up there for a few seconds while you disappear below, and the zombie will not matter to either of you once you are gone. So it stops, and comes down with you. The same goes for a tree it had started on or a pot it was heading for: work you are walking away from loses its point the moment you walk away, and nothing it had already started makes it worth being left behind for.

There is a way to get this wrong, and it matters that the companion does not. Reading where you are heading is not predicting where you will be in five seconds and racing there. If you are walking right and the companion is on your left, and you turn left for a moment to grab something, it does not go charging away to the left: it is already on your left, it is already where that brief turn was pointing, and a moment's turn is not a change of plan. It simply keeps moving with you, and when you resume right, nothing has been lost and no ground has opened up between you. It hurries only when it is genuinely far from where you are going.

There is a second way to get it wrong, and it is the one a fast body invites. Being quick enough to be anywhere is not a licence to be everywhere: it does not orbit you, it does not overshoot your every turn and swing back, and it does not hover exactly in the tile you are about to walk into. It does not settle on a spot at all. It moves along with you inside the space around you, carrying your pace, and wanders through that space in slow, unhurried sweeps (the front of it, then the middle, up over you, down beside you), so that watching it is restful rather than frantic, and nothing about it ever looks like catching up.

#### 6:00: The cave, and going in without you

There is a hole in the ground ahead and the inside of it is dark. The companion is aware of that darkness the way it is aware of the trees (as a place with a property, twelve tiles wide and unlit, sitting over there) rather than as something it notices only once it is inside it. Its light is off on the bright surface, because there is nothing here to light; the torch comes out as the light fails at the lip, and it goes away again the moment its surroundings are bright, whether the light is a torch it placed, one you are carrying, or the sun.

You keep walking along the surface and go straight past the hole. **The companion goes in anyway.** It drops in, lights the first chamber, and then, because the passage beyond it is dark too, it goes on and lights that, and the pocket off it, one dark patch after the next, and it comes back out to catch up with you only when there is nothing left down there worth lighting. At no point did it look to you for a decision. Had you gone in instead, it would already have been down there ahead of you doing the same thing. Keeping the place lit is its own responsibility and it discharges it whether or not you are participating; you have never once had to place a torch to make it place one.

This really highlights the agency of the companion. It does not simply follow you around; it makes decisions of its own. At this moment it weighed its options and decided that going into the cave to light it would be within budget and would not stray from what it thought your intended action was. Even if your intended action did stray from what the companion was doing, it was fine, because the companion weighs the options and decides that it has the time, regardless of what decision you might make.

#### 8:00: Crevices, and who gets the light first

It never runs out of torches. The ones it puts up are its own, so a long cave gets lit end to end without you keeping it stocked, and none of yours are used. If you have handed it a stack of a particular kind, bone or coloured torches say, that is the kind it puts up.

This time you do go down, and you head along a cave system that runs mostly sideways, with narrow side passages branching off it into the dark every so often. The companion ducks into them. It slips in, lights what is in there, and comes back out to rejoin you. It fits wherever you would fit and a little more, so a passage two tiles wide is a passage, and a crack one tile wide is a wall to it the way it is to you; it does not press at a crack and it does not try to squeeze. Because it flies, there is no such thing as a passage it can get into and not out of, so it never has to make the crevice-with-no-way-back judgement that a walker would; what it weighs instead is time, danger and how far you are getting.

Which dark it deals with first depends on you, and it reads that sensibly. If you are down here carrying nothing that gives off light, standing in your own small patch of black, then you are the dark that matters and the crevices can wait; the thing that gets someone killed is not seeing the skeleton beside them. If you are carrying a torch or you have thrown a glowstick ahead of you, your own bubble is handled, and the companion takes itself off on a proper expedition: the crevices, the corridor ahead, the chamber you have not walked into yet. Either way what it is pursuing is the same thing: that this cave is lit. And it means lit when you are gone, not lit while you stand there: the stretch of corridor that is bright only because you are walking through it with a torch in your hand is still on its list, and so is the ground under a light pet that will follow you out. Your light, its own light and the pet's light are all things that leave; a placed torch, a patch of lava and the sun are not, and it never confuses the two. Anywhere in a dark stretch that you could put a torch yourself (the spot your own smart cursor would offer you), it can put one too, and it does, coming down to the wall or the floor where a torch can actually go rather than lighting only what happens to be in reach of where it hovers. A dark place it leaves unlit is one it has a reason to leave, never one it failed to notice.

Partway along, the corridor runs into a wooden door somebody built into the rock a long time ago. It does not stop, and it does not treat the door as the end of the corridor: it opens it, slips through, and the door closes behind it, because that is simply what going through a door is. It is specific about which doors those are: one with rock hard against both sides cannot swing anywhere and is a wall wearing a door's shape, and the companion reads it as a wall and goes round; one with room on its far side opens and is fine. It plans on the answer rather than on the assumption, because pressing at a door that was never going to move is exactly the kind of thing this companion does not do.

Twenty tiles further on there is a fourth passage, and this one goes down a shaft to a pool. Water is nothing to the companion (it flies through a flooded shaft exactly as it flies through an empty one), so it goes down past the waterline and lights the shaft to the bottom, putting torches wherever there is dry wall to take one, because a passage lit only to the water is a passage half lit.

Change what is in the passage and the answer moves again. Suppose there is something alive down there: a bat, say, awake and coming up toward the mouth. Now there are two reasons to deal with that passage rather than one, and both point the same way, so it deals with it: it backs off to where it has a clear line up the shaft and kills the bat on its way out, then lights what it can see. But suppose instead the thing down there is a jellyfish in the pool. The water is no obstacle, but the jellyfish is: it stings anything that comes close, and it cannot leave the water. So the companion lights the shaft, shoots the jellyfish from the air above the pool where it cannot be reached, and only then goes down to light what is under the surface.

Each passage competes with moving with you, lighting the corridor ahead, dealing with the bat and staying somewhere safe. Darkness matters alongside the effort, danger and distance. The same passage can be worth entering at one moment and worth leaving alone a moment later because the rest of the scene has changed.

#### 10:00: Copper, and being sure

There is copper in the wall to the left, six tiles up, and the companion breaks off what it was doing and goes for it, not because ore is sacred, but because a vein it can actually get to is worth more than another torch right now.

It does not confuse seeing copper with being able to work on it. Where the way is clear it flies to the vein and hangs beside it, and the beam comes out. Copper behind solid dirt with no permitted way to reach it remains in the wall: it will not dig a tunnel to get there, and it will not pretend the dirt is not there. Copper across a gap is copper it simply flies to, and copper above a ledge is copper it simply rises to; the old questions of whether it can reach, climb or get back do not arise, and what remains is whether the trip is worth the time and the danger.

Once it is there it drills until the vein is gone, and when the last piece of it is out it dips and picks up what fell. You start on the other end of the same vein and it keeps helping. Copper you have already exposed or partly broken matters just as much as copper it worked on itself; what remains to be done is the useful part. Back among the trees it is different: while you cut one trunk, it usually chooses another, so the two of you make progress together without crowding the same tree.

It does not shoot on the way over, and it does not shoot while it drills. Its weapons are for fighting, and fighting is something it switches into: a passing enemy worth killing turns the trip into a fight, and one that is not worth it is left alone. Once it has started drilling, the beam stays on the work through the rhythm of the cuts. A real interruption (something coming for you, or something it can hurt close enough to matter) makes it stop the beam and fight, then come back to the vein when that makes sense. Its torch is less demanding: it can put the light away for a fight and take it out again afterwards without turning that small courtesy into another job.

Getting out of the way is not an interruption at all. An arrow or a bat coming at it while it drills does not end the job: it curves off the line, lets the thing go past, and is back beside the ore with the beam on, the vein never having stopped being what it was doing. The same holds while it follows you or fights: staying unhurt rides on top of whatever it is busy with, and it is never a second job that replaces the first. A companion that dropped everything and hid each time something moved near it would be a companion that did nothing else, and a zombie standing next to it is no reason to stay put while you walk away. Nor does a way out of harm ever end with it pressed against a wall going nowhere: a slime bouncing about at the far side of a pool is not something to flinch from, and a move that dodges one bat by pinning it against the rock is not a move at all.

The next piece of copper falls off the far edge into a deep pit. Breaking it has not obliged the companion to follow it. A pit is not a trap for it, so what decides is only whether the trip down and back is worth the piece: if a fight has started or you are leaving the area, it leaves the drop below and moves on; if nothing else is happening, it dips, takes it and returns in a second.

There is more copper across the chamber, and between here and there the chamber is flooded to the ceiling for six tiles. That changes nothing for the companion. It flies through the water as through air, hangs beside the vein on the far side and drills it, and comes back the same way with what fell.

Then you walk over and knock out two blocks so a wall of rock opens onto a second vein, and within a second or two the companion is on its way through the gap. Nobody told it the world had changed and it was not sitting there hoping. It re-asks what is worth doing constantly, and what is worth doing is a function of what is actually there right now, so a wall coming down and an enemy arriving and a vein running out are all the same kind of event to it: the answer to "what now" came back different from the last time it asked.

The same thing runs the other way. Halfway to a vein it can reach, a skeleton walks into the corridor between it and the ore. The companion does not carry on to the vein and get hit on the way, and it also does not treat the interruption as the end of the job: it deals with the skeleton from above, where a skeleton cannot reach, and then, because the vein is still there and still worth having and it is still nearly on top of it, it carries on and drills it. Had the skeleton instead been three of them, and you in the corridor with them, the vein would have lost outright and it would have gone to you and fought from there. Neither of those is a rule about skeletons. They are the same question asked twice with different numbers in it.

The part it is strict about is the difference between *no* and *not sure yet*. Where the way is genuinely unknown (a dark stretch it has not looked at, a region it has not worked out yet), it does not fly at a maybe; it does something it already knows it can do and looks again a moment later. A new opening can change the answer later, so leaving the vein alone now does not mean forgetting it forever.

#### 12:00: One thing after another

You are at a chest and stationary. Ahead of the companion, in a line, there is a pot on a ledge, an unlit corner above the pot, and a vein ten tiles past both. It wants the vein, and getting to the vein takes it right past the other two.

So it breaks the pot on the way, with whatever weapon it has (an arrow if it has a bow, a swipe if it has a sword), because the possible contents are worth the small effort here. The contents drop and it scoops them up without slowing. The corner is a tile above the line, so it rises and lights it. Then it arrives at the vein and starts drilling. Each small diversion was worthwhile because the journey was already being made. If the vein is gone but the pot is still nearby, a short trip for its possible contents can be worthwhile on its own. If those contents would fall into water, or its bag is full, the same pot can be left alone. Terraria is a game of doing three things on the way to a fourth, and the companion plays it that way while remaining free to change its mind.

It is thinking a stretch ahead, the way a person is: not only the slime in front of it, but the corner after, the vein after that, and what it will do on the way back. Order matters as much as choice, and the order is a plan it is following, not a preference it re-picks every moment. Put a slime between the companion and a dark corner it wants to light, and it does not fly past the slime to light the corner and then come back for it: it kills the slime on the way, picks up what it drops right there, and then lights the corner, because in that order it is working almost the whole time and flying the least. Move the slime to the far side of the corner and the order flips (corner first, slime after) for the same reason. Seven unlit corners in one chamber are the same question: it works them in an order, and it does not light the nearest, fly to the new nearest, and recross what it just lit. Three creatures are the same question inside a fight.

Once it has set off on one of those orders it does not flip-flop halfway. Being partway there is part of what makes the nearer job cheaper (the flight already spent is spent), so two jobs that were always about even do not swap, and it only changes course when something genuinely better turns up, including what it would cost to switch. A new creature walking in can be that something, if dealing with it now is cheaper than finishing and then dealing with it. The same two jobs swapping with each other, nothing new having arrived, is the thing it does not do. And dropping is sometimes the cheaper course: you walk off down a cave while it is finishing a slime, and if by the time the slime is dead you are far enough that coming back is the whole of the remaining job, it leaves the slime and comes. A slime one hit from dead, with you still nearby, it finishes.

What sits on the way is the same kind of leftover whatever it is. A torch on the path home, a slime on the path home, a drop on the path home, two blocks of copper that take as long as flying past them: each is worth doing if doing it now and then carrying on is cheaper than flying past and coming back, and none of them is a special courtesy of lighting. Two copper it takes; a vein that would outlast the trip home it compares against coming back, because there may be work around you, and it does not stay for the vein out of stubbornness and it does not skip the two blocks out of hurry.

The same idea decides which way it goes at all. Off to the left there is a single slime and a drop. Off to the right there is one enemy and one unlit corner. Straight ahead there is a stretch with four dark spots, two pots and something alive in it. It goes straight ahead, not because lighting is more important than killing, and not because pots are more important than slimes, but because that is where the evening's work is sitting together. Put the same four dark spots behind a wall of rock with no way through, and it takes the smaller place it can reach. Start walking away while it is in there, and catching you up can beat finishing every last corner. The place with more to do is worth more only while getting there, doing it and getting back still makes sense.

Halfway through the vein a bat comes in through a gap in the ceiling and makes for you. The companion stops mid-cut (the cut is not sacred either), kills the bat, and goes straight back to the same vein and finishes it. That return is seamless. It does not hang there reconsidering its life, and it does not wander off to something else on the way back; the vein was the best thing available before the bat and it still is.

It matters what it does *not* do on that trip out. It does not pass the pot and make an unnecessary second journey for it later. It does not return to you between every small action. And being interested in ore does not make a useful nearby pot or dark corner somebody else's problem. Those diversions remain choices about what they add to the journey, rather than obligations to collect and finish everything it has noticed.

Move the pot and the answer changes without anything else changing. Put it twenty tiles off the route and the companion passes it by, not because pots stop being worth breaking at some distance, but because that pot now costs a trip and a trip back and the ore does not. Move it back onto the path and it is worth it again. The unlit corner behaves the same way: a tile above the route it takes it, twenty tiles off to the side down a dead end it leaves it, and if you stand in that dead end a moment later it becomes worth lighting again because now there is a second reason.

Change the timing instead of the geometry. If the bat had come in while the companion was still on its way to the vein rather than mid-cut, the pot and the torch would simply not have happened: it would have turned and dealt with the bat from wherever it was, and picked the journey up afterwards with whatever was still on it. If the bat had come in and you had killed it yourself before the companion got there, it would never have broken stride at all. And if by the time it finished with the bat you had walked forty tiles further on and the vein was now well behind it, the vein would have lost to catching up with you and it would have left the copper in the wall without any sense of having failed at something.

Change how much work remains. With five pieces of ore left and a dangerous zombie approaching, it stops the beam because finishing would take too long. With one easy piece left and the zombie still distant, it can finish before the trouble arrives. Give both of you enough protection that the creature would barely scratch you, and there may be no reason to interrupt at all. Send a serious threat towards you instead and helping you can matter more than the nearly finished ore. The difference is what another moment of work will cost, not pride in how much work it has already done.

You take two steps right, turn back for something on the ground, then cross left and right again while sorting your bag. The companion carries on with the nearby corner and drop; a brief change of direction does not send it abandoning and reclaiming them. Then you actually descend into the cave. It leaves work that would keep it behind and comes with you, taking a quick useful thing on the way only when it can still catch you comfortably. If you return before it has left the chamber, what remains there is available again, without an obligation to finish the old trip.

Its next shot was meant to shove a slime into a line with another, but you strike the slime first and send it somewhere else. The arrow already flying stays an arrow already flying. The companion takes its next shot at where the creatures now are, and uses the new opening if your hit made one. If a creature suddenly leaps or vanishes to the other side of the chamber, it adjusts again. It does not keep arranging a fight around a hit that never landed, and it does not wait empty-handed for the creatures to return to the arrangement it wanted. The same judgement applies when you take the drop, break the next piece of copper or light the corner yourself: work already done by either of you does not need doing twice.

#### 14:00: The slimes it was ignoring

Three slimes have been audible for a while in a chamber off to the side, and the companion has done nothing about them the whole time, because they cannot get out and it has been busy with things that were worth more. Now the vein is gone, the drops are collected, the corner is lit, there is nothing else on the floor, and you are still standing at your chest. With everything better used up, the slimes are now the best thing there is, and the companion goes and deals with them.

It gets itself somewhere it can shoot from (above them, out of a slime's reach, which for a slime is nearly anywhere), and starts putting damage in. They bounce at it, and it drifts up and back as they come, and it kills all three without taking a hit. It collects what they drop. Then it looks around again, finds a lit cave with nothing on the floor, no ore, nothing alive and a player who has not moved, and comes back to hang near you. Nobody told it to do any of that in any particular order; the order fell out of what each thing was worth at the time.

The interesting thing here is the two minutes when it did nothing about them, because that is the part that usually reads as broken and is in fact the point. It was aware of those slimes the whole time. It did not forget them, it was not unable to see them, and it was not waiting for a signal. They were simply worth less than a vein of copper and less than an unlit chamber, and a thing worth less than the thing you are doing is a thing you do not do. The moment everything above them was gone they rose to the top without any change in how it felt about slimes. Staying near you was never one of the things they were competing with: hanging about beside you is what it does when there is nothing else to do at all, so a slime ten or fifteen tiles above you on a quiet afternoon is worth going up for, and so is one at the far edge of the screen once nothing nearer is left.

Change the circumstances and the timing moves. If one of those slimes had found its way out of the chamber and started bouncing toward you while the companion was still drilling, it would have left the vein for that one and gone back to the vein afterwards, not because a slime outranks copper, but because a slime that is arriving is a completely different thing from a slime that is sitting in a sealed room. If you had got up from the chest and started walking on while it was fighting them, it would have finished the fight, because being near you is never a reason to drop work; only if you had walked beyond the distance it works from you would it leave the slimes that were left and come after you, the fast way if it was about to lose you altogether, and the first thing it picked once the last slime died would be whatever lies along the way you are now heading. And if the chamber had been sealed in a way it could never open, the slimes would have stayed at the bottom of its list for ever and it would have gone and found something else to do rather than pressing at the wall.

#### 16:00: Everything at once

Terraria does not hand you one problem at a time, and this is the part that matters most.

You are in the jungle, standing on ore. The chamber is poorly lit. A hornet is up on the top left, a snapping eater down on the bottom left, two jungle slimes dropping toward you, a Demon Eye and three zombies off to the right. That eye is the ordinary night flyer, not the Eye of Cthulhu; this is not a boss fight. Hearts, mana stars and ordinary drops are on the floor and in the air. Pots sit on ledges. Projectiles are coming from more than one side.

The companion does not freeze, and it does not pick the single most dangerous creature and shoot only that. It has a stretch of work and it starts it. The slimes are the ones about to land on you, so those go first. What they drop it scoops as it rises, because it is already there. It climbs until the Demon Eye and the three zombies share a line, knocks the eye down onto them, and throws through the line. The hornet is the one that will come in if it is left, so it is next, and on the way back toward the fight it puts torches on the dark wall it is already flying along (not as a new job, as leftover on a path it is taking anyway), and it is in position to throw again before the zombies have spread. Both zombies go. It dives for the drops and lights as it picks, because those two sit together. Then it works the snapping eater from a line that also closes on the drops along that wall, and while it does that it is off every projectile that would have hit it, off the eater's reach, off the hornet thorns still in the air. When the eater is dead it goes to the ore, not to the last drop, because that drop is going to fall on it. It drills.

A new slime drops from the top of the screen while it is still on the vein. It does not finish the vein with the slime on it, and it does not abandon the vein for a long fight. A shove from the bow it is holding knocks the slime off you and off it; the shurikens finish it; the drops come up; the beam goes back on the ore. The other weapon first, or the ore first with the slime landing, or a long hover waiting for a perfect line, are the stretches it does not take.

Shift one thing and the stretch changes inside the same decision. The slimes are still distant and the Demon Eye is already on you: the eye is first, the slimes wait, and the knock onto the zombies still happens if the eye is the one that can be lined up. The ore is two hits from done and nothing is about to land: it finishes the ore, then fights, because two hits is shorter than starting a fight it would come back from. A dummy that cannot hurt anyone is in the room with the ore: it is not a fight, it is scenery, and the ore is still the work.

The same stretch is how it weighs coming back to you against working here against working on the way. If you have gone, and the remaining fight would outlast the trip, it comes. If two copper sit on the path to you and take as long as flying past them, it takes them on the way. If a vein here would keep it while you are already somewhere else with work around you, it leaves the vein. None of those is a courtesy that only lighting gets, and none of them is a rule that fighting always comes first.

Three things it did not do in that chamber are as important as what it did. It did not stand still choosing the single best shot while slimes landed and drops sat and the wall stayed dark. It did not swap, halfway through the Demon Eye, to the nearest slime and back again because the two looked about even from where it now was. And it did not, once the last body was dead, recross the chamber for a drop it could have taken while it was there, or fly home past two copper it could have drilled in passing.

Now the same idea in a smaller room. You are up on a dry ledge on the left. Below and to the right is a water pool with two jellyfish in it, there is unmined ore in the floor of that pool, two zombies are closing from the right on the far side of the water, and the whole area is half-lit. The companion was drilling the ore from inside the pool, hovering beside it under the surface, when a jellyfish surged and stung it, so it is now in the pool within reach of two jellyfish, with zombies inbound and you dry and safe twenty tiles away.

It does not freeze and it does not try to finish what it started. It leaves (straight up out of the water, then to the left, up to the air over your ledge), and it does that not because you are there but because that is the only place in this scene where nothing can reach it. Hanging beside the ore means being stung by two jellyfish, and a copper vein is not worth that. Once it is out of the water, the jellyfish stop being a problem at all, instantly and completely, because jellyfish cannot leave water and the companion is aware of that. It turns and starts shooting the zombies as they come, giving ground to the left to keep the gap open.

Then a bat comes in from the left, behind it, and it is pinched. What it does here is the whole character of the thing. It kills the bat first, not because bats are more dangerous than zombies, but because the bat is the one thing in this scene that can actually reach it in the air, and a zombie underneath a flying orb is a zombie that will never connect. Set the same scene slightly differently (the bat still distant, the zombies about to reach you), and it kills the zombies instead, because now you are the one about to be hit. Same reasoning, opposite action, and nothing in it was decided in advance.

Put a zombie between the companion and you, and helping you does not mean flying through it: it goes over, which for the companion is simply the way from here to there, and shoots down on the way. If there is no useful intervention from safety, it weighs the real damage and the danger you face rather than treating your position as somewhere it must reach at any cost.

With the bat dead and the zombies down it hangs above the pool and shoots the jellyfish dead from the air. With the pool safe it goes back down into the water, drills the ore it came for from beside it, and collects the lot on its way back up: jellyfish drops, zombie drops, the bat, the ore. Then it comes back to you.

Three things it did not do are as important as what it did. It did not finish the ore while the jellyfish were on it. It had wanted it thirty seconds earlier, and it left it, because a vein is worth a fixed amount and being stung is not, and no amount of being partway through something makes finishing it worth more than it is worth. It did not run to you for safety, either; it ended up over your ledge, but it went there because that air was out of reach of everything, and it would have gone to an identical spot in the opposite direction just as readily. And it did not stop shooting while it was retreating. Climbing out of the water, it was still putting arrows into whatever it had a line to.

Now change the water. Make the pool jellyfish-free and most of this scene evaporates: the water itself costs nothing, so the ore is worth finishing from inside the pool, and the zombies arriving change that only when they arrive.

Change who is in the water instead. If *you* had dropped into that pool with two jellyfish and the companion were above, it would not dive in beside you. It would shoot the jellyfish from the air, where it can hit them and they cannot hit it, and do that faster than anything else, because the thing hurting you outranks the thing hurting it. Joining you at the jellyfish's level would put a second body in reach of the same stings and give up the angle the problem is solved from.

And change the bat's arrival to a moment later. If it had come in after the zombies were already dead, the companion would simply have shot it from where it hung and gone back to the ore, and none of the climbing and repositioning would have happened at all. The bat did not decide the whole stretch; it decided whether the stretch had a hole in it. When it arrived in the middle of the retreat it was the next step, because it could actually connect; when it arrived after, it was leftover on the way back to the ore. The companion did not throw the rest of the evening away to start again from nothing, and it did not ignore the bat to finish a plan that no longer fitted. That is the character of the whole scene: a stretch of work it is in the middle of, willing to change when something genuinely cheaper or more urgent turns up, and unwilling to dither between two things that were always about even.

#### 18:00: The shaft it should not have entered

You jump down a shaft and it comes down with you. At the bottom is water right across the shaft and you land in it. It comes straight on into the pool, because water is no more to it than air, and it stays with you there exactly as it would on dry ground, fighting anything in the water with you and lighting the shaft while you are down.

What the companion is willing to go into is decided by what can hurt it, and no liquid can: water, honey, lava and shimmer are all air to its body, and it has always been that way rather than something it earns. Every decision that asks "can I be there" answers the same way about a flooded shaft as about an empty one, and it never plans as though a pool were a wall.

Its growth follows yours as well as its own practice. When you become faster it becomes faster in step, so it is never left behind by a new pair of boots; when you gain more reach it drills from further; when your defence rises so does its own. It uses the extra speed and reach in ordinary decisions immediately: a higher piece of ore can be worked from further off, a torch can go somewhere it could not before. Better protection also changes which small dangers are worth interrupting work for. Losing an advantage matters just as promptly; it does not attempt yesterday's flight with today's slower body.

#### 20:00: The wall of lava

You are working along a tunnel and it opens into a chamber whose floor is lava, with ore glinting in the wall on the far side. The companion crosses. Lava does not hurt it, so the lake is simply the air over a room: it flies over or through it, drills the ore, and comes back with the drops.

Change the geometry and the answer moves only for the reasons it moves anywhere else: how far the ore is, what else is worth doing, and what is alive in the chamber. A bat circling over the lake is the one thing here that can hurt it, so it deals with the bat or waits for it to go, and the lava plays no part in that decision.

The thing it must never be is idle and in danger at the same time.

#### 22:00: Somewhere it cannot follow

You take a route it cannot take: a squeeze through a one-tile gap it does not fit, a door it cannot open. It does not hang at the gap trying the same impossible move. Useful things remain nearby (trees, a slime, drops on the floor, a dark corner), and it can get on with them while you are still close enough that this makes sense. Distance matters throughout, alongside danger and the work remaining. If you keep moving away beyond the gap, rejoining you can become more important while trees are still standing, and it tracks you along the nearest open way as best it can; if you remain nearby, it need not abandon useful work merely because it cannot be beside you.

The clearest version of this used to be the floating island, and for the companion the island is not a problem at all: you fly up and it flies up with you, and if it is a long way it takes a moment longer and arrives. What it cannot follow is a passage too narrow for its body. A flooded tunnel is not one of those: if you swim under a flooded wall it swims it with you. Where there is genuinely nothing on this side of the gap (a bare stone corridor with no trees, no enemies, nothing on the floor and nothing dark), then the distance to you is the only thing left on the table, and it acts on that immediately rather than after a delay, waiting as close to you as the rock allows. There is no pointless pause before it tries to rejoin you. "Nothing to do" and "go to the player" are the same answer arrived at from two directions.

An enemy arriving changes it back. Something wanders into the corridor while you are still underwater and the companion deals with it, then returns to the question. That is not it forgetting about you; the distance to you was the best thing available for as long as nothing better existed, and something better existed for thirty seconds.

#### 24:00: Home, and getting out of the way

You head back with a full bag and it comes with you. The way home is still a path with leftover on it, and leftover is not only loot. A drop along the route, a slime, an unlit corner, two blocks of copper that take as long as the flight past them: each is worth a dip if the dip is cheaper than coming back for it later, and each is left if you are hurrying, if a fight is on you, or if the leftover would outlast the trip. Even a single block of dirt can be worth two seconds. It need not know the importance of every item to your playthrough to help carry it. Put that same block at the bottom of a pool, or make the leftover a vein that would keep it down there while you are already home, and it leaves it behind. Giving ordinary work a chance on a path it is already flying is different from promising to finish every job it has noticed, and it is also different from lighting being the one thing allowed on the way back.

When your inventory cannot take a drop, its own bag can carry it. When neither of you has room, it leaves the item where it is and makes the lack of space clear; the item does not vanish and the companion does not keep making the same fruitless trip. Free some space or change what lies nearby, and collecting can become useful again.

At home you start sinking a shaft (the two-wide platform drop straight down), and the companion is hanging exactly where the next platform goes. It moves. While you are holding something you can place and your cursor is anywhere near it, that cursor is a place it stays out of, so it drifts up, or sideways, and it keeps doing that as you work your way down the shaft, out of the way each time before you click rather than after.

Swap what is in your hand and it stops caring. Holding a sword, a pickaxe or a torch, it hangs wherever it likes, because none of those are blocked by a body being in the way and getting shoved around by your cursor for no reason would be its own kind of annoying. The thing it is reacting to is not your cursor; it is the fact that you are about to put something where it is.

The same courtesy is wider than building. It is never the thing hanging in the one-tile-tall gap you are walking through (it is above the gap or through it first), and in a doorway it goes through ahead of you or waits on its own side, rather than meeting you in the frame. At home it comes in through the door with you and finds a corner of the room to hang in, out of the line between you and your chests. None of that is a set of rules about doorways and shafts; it is one idea (being where you are trying to be is a cost) applied everywhere it comes up.

There is a version of this that would be worse than not having it. If it took the idea too seriously it would spend the whole session skittering away from you, unable to hang anywhere because you might want to be there. What stops that is that being in your way is a small cost rather than a prohibition: it is enough to move it out of the tile you are about to fill, and nowhere near enough to make it abandon a good firing position because you glanced in that direction.

#### 26:00: The eye

Night falls and you use the Suspicious Looking Eye. The music changes and the Eye of Cthulhu comes down out of the dark.

Everything else the companion was interested in stops mattering. It is not drilling, it is not lighting anything, it is not sweeping the floor, and it is not hanging near you, because nothing else in the world is worth anything while this thing is alive. It has two concerns now and they are in a strict order: **do not get hit, then do damage.** In that order, and the order is not negotiable. If the only way to get the boss in range is to fly somewhere it will take a hit, it does not fly there; it stays out and does less damage, because it cannot do any damage at all once it is on the floor.

It also stops trying to look after you, and that is correct. Ordinarily, when something comes for you, the companion makes a judgement: that zombie is heading for him, but he can handle a zombie, so I will finish this vein and step in if it turns serious. Against a boss that judgement is meaningless, because there is no version of this where it hangs between you and the Eye of Cthulhu and that helps. So it drops the idea entirely and fights.

It also stops being tethered to you the way it normally is. Boss arenas are enormous; a companion held on a short leash spends the whole fight bobbing around your shoulder instead of fighting. It ranges much further than it ever would ordinarily, and higher, with a gentle pull back that gets stronger the further out it goes, so it has room to manoeuvre without ever genuinely leaving. The same is true whenever the game itself turns into an event (a Blood Moon, a Solar Eclipse, a Goblin Invasion, a Pumpkin Moon), because those are the same situation: the world is now about one thing, and hanging next to you being helpful is not it.

What it does not set down is you being in trouble for reasons it *can* act on: if the small eyes swarm you specifically, they are things it can shoot and they are on you, and it shoots those before it goes back to the boss. Nothing else survives the fight either. It does not drill the arena wall because there is copper in it. It does not stop to light a dark corner. It does not pick up the drops until the fight is over: they are not going anywhere, and a detour for loot mid-fight is how a fight goes wrong.

If you die halfway through, it does not stop fighting and it does not hang over your gravestone. The boss is still alive, it is still the only thing that matters, and it keeps going for as long as it can, which is often long enough to matter, because a boss that is nearly dead when you respawn is a very different fight from one at full health.

Take the same evening and replace the boss with a Blood Moon and very little changes. There is no single enormous thing to shoot, but the world is still about one thing, so the companion is still fighting rather than drilling, still ranging further than usual, and still prioritising its own survival over the last few points of damage. What tells it this is happening is the state of the world rather than the identity of the monster, which is why an invasion from a mod it has never heard of produces the same behaviour.

#### 28:00: The fight

It fights the way a decent player with wings fights. It stays at the range where it can hit and not be hit, and it keeps moving. When the Eye stops circling and lines itself up for a charge, the companion is out of that line before the charge lands, not by recognising this particular boss, which it has never heard of, but by seeing something aiming itself and accelerating and getting off the line it is aiming along. Off the line, for a thing that flies, is usually up or down rather than back, because back along the charge is the one direction that does not work. That works the first time it meets an attack nobody has ever described to it.

When the Eye splits and starts throwing out a swarm of small eyes, it changes weapon, and it changes weapon for the right reason. Something that punches through a line of targets is worth far more against a swarm than against one large thing, and something that hits one target very hard is the reverse. It is looking at the two weapons you gave it, the targets and how those things are arranged. Hand it a different pair and the same judgement considers what those two can actually do. It does not take a weapon out of your inventory and pretend to know how it works: what it uses is what you put in its hands, and it learns each one by using it: the first few shots with a new weapon can go wide while it gets the feel of how that thing flies, and after that it aims like it has always had it.

A staff in its hands is the same story with one more thing to watch. Magic tires it: the more it casts without a pause, the weaker each cast lands, down to about half, and it never refuses to cast. So a staff that started the fight as the better weapon can become the worse one twenty seconds in, and when it does the companion swaps to the bow, not because a rule said so, but because the bow is now the weapon doing more. Give it a moment and the staff comes back.

Its own survival keeps winning ties all the way through. At full health with a clear line it is aggressive, sits at the edge of its range and fires constantly. At a third health it is noticeably more careful (wider standoff, quicker to break off, more willing to spend a second getting somewhere safe rather than squeezing in another shot), not because a threshold was crossed but because getting hit costs more when there is less left. If you are at full health and it is nearly dead, it looks after itself, because you can take a hit and it cannot, and a companion that dies to prove a point contributes nothing for the rest of the fight.

If it does go down mid-fight, it is back in the air within ten seconds and rejoins, so a bad moment costs you its help for ten seconds rather than for the rest of the fight.

#### 30:00: After

The Eye dies and the loot scatters across the arena. The companion sweeps down, collects what it can reach and brings it over.

The return to ordinary is immediate and complete. There is no cooldown, no winding down, no hanging still for a moment: the enormous thing that was outweighing everything else simply is not there any more, and the next-best thing on the pile takes over, which in a boss arena at night is usually the loot and then the fact that half the arena is unlit. Within a few seconds it is a companion again: hovering near you, looking around, and if there is a tree or a dark corner or a vein anywhere close, it has already gone to deal with it.

Summon the next boss thirty seconds later and it drops everything again just as fast, mid-cut if it is mid-cut. The unfinished work can wait. When the fight ends, it returns to whatever is then useful, without a needless pause or an obligation to recreate the interrupted evening.

That is the whole character of it: useful intentions without stubborn obligations. It can remember the vein it left, finish work that is still worth finishing, and abandon it when the world has changed. It acts coherently enough to look deliberate and adapts readily enough to remain helpful. How it reaches those decisions does not change what the player should experience.

### Behaviour By Behaviour: one row per responsibility, the specification the story narrates

Each row names one thing the companion is responsible for and what it should do in play, written so it stays true whatever is built underneath it. The story above narrates these rows; where the two disagree, the row wins.

| Behaviour | Expected |
|---|---|
| **Getting out of the player's way**, not occupying space the player is trying to use | It is never standing in the tile the player is about to place a block in, and never in the gap the player is walking through. It gets out before the click rather than after, and it only cares while the player holds something placeable; holding a sword or a torch it stands wherever it likes. The cost of being in the way is small rather than absolute, so it never skitters away from a good position because the player glanced in that direction. |
| **Boss and event behaviour**, how it plays when the world is about one thing | During a boss or a world event it drops everything else, stops trying to protect the player because that judgement is meaningless against a boss, ranges far wider than it ever would ordinarily, held by a pull back that strengthens with distance rather than by a wall it cannot cross, and optimises its own survival first and damage second. It still shoots things that are specifically on the player. It keeps fighting if the player dies. The same applies to a Blood Moon or an invasion from a mod nobody has written anything for, because what triggers it is the state of the world rather than the identity of the monster. |
| **Reading where the player is going**, moving with a heading rather than toward a position | It moves with the player's direction and local activity, without pretending to know their future destination. Being level with the player or ahead on their side, a little above, is the ordinary case, because it is much faster than they are; trailing is what happens when it stopped for something, never a policy. A brief turn does not make it race past them, and its speed is not a licence to orbit, overshoot or hover in the tile they are about to walk into. The space around the player leans the way they are going and always still contains them, so it never runs to the edge of the screen, and the companion does not aim for a point in it: it keeps moving through the whole of that space at roughly the player's pace, easing to the front, falling back toward the middle, rising and dipping, so it is never behind them and never flies to a spot, stops, drifts out and flies back. Sustained rapid travel discourages optional excursions, while moving around one cave chamber to place torches still leaves time for independent nearby work. The pull back toward the player applies whatever it is doing and is measured from that space rather than from the player: none inside it, growing as the gap opens, never on its own outweighing a job genuinely worth doing, and taking all of its attention only at the distance where it would lose them altogether. A brief useful job can still justify stopping during travel when it can finish and comfortably catch up; changed terrain, danger or the player's pace can change that judgement before the job is finished. Brief left/right reversals while the player remains nearby do not make it repeatedly abandon and reclaim useful work; an actual departure still changes what is worth finishing immediately. |
| **Enemy selection**, which of the things it could shoot it actually shoots | Of everything it could hit, it picks what is worth hitting: what is attacking the player, what is about to reach it, what it can finish. When two things will both land and there is not time to stop both, it prefers the hit it would rather take, and a tap on the slower one, then deleting the one about to connect, then finishing the first, is an ordinary sequence once both are in reach. Which of three creatures dies first is a stretch of moves, not three separate choices: knocking one into the others and throwing through the line can beat deleting the most dangerous in place. A dummy that cannot hurt anyone is not in that stretch. Something it cannot hit from anywhere it can reach is not a candidate for walking toward. Walking toward a creature does not lock that creature as the shot: which body and which weapon are one choice from where it stands right now, because a piercing shot through three things can beat a harder hit on one, and a weapon that goes through walls does not need anyone to walk. It shoots only while it is fighting: while it mines, lights, loots or keeps you company its weapons stay quiet, and something worth shooting is a reason to start fighting, which it does readily whenever an enemy it can hurt is close. It does not treat a long fight as a reason to ignore a servant. It minds where its hits shove what they hit: it never knocks a creature into the player or into itself when a shot from another side, or with the other weapon, would push it away, and it charges a shot only for the danger the shove adds, not for danger that creature was bringing anyway. Hurting something dangerous counts even when the hit does not kill it. If the player hits a creature first, or the creature jumps, dashes or suddenly appears elsewhere, the companion uses the situation that actually resulted. It does not require its earlier shot to have landed, and it does not wait for the old arrangement to return before making a useful attack. |
| **Chaining several jobs into one trip**, doing what is on the way, on the way | A pot, an unlit corner and a vein along the same journey can be dealt with efficiently together. Their extra travel, danger and delay decide whether each is worth doing, and the order it does them in is chosen the same way: a slime on the way to a dark corner is killed, its drops collected, and then the corner lit, because that order has it working sooner and flying less than lighting first and coming back. Seven unlit corners, three creatures, a fight then a pickup then a torch: the same question, not a special case of lighting. What sits on a path it is already flying, a torch, a slime, a drop, two copper that take as long as flying past, is leftover of any kind, including on the way home, and is taken when doing it now is cheaper than coming back, never as a courtesy only lighting gets. A long job is judged by how much it gets done per second rather than by how long the whole of it would take, so a big vein is not passed over for being big. An interruption or the player moving away can change what remains worth finishing; the journey never becomes an obligation to perform every earlier opportunity. |
| **Playing several steps ahead**, having a stretch of work and following it, including inside a fight | It designates a stretch of work and follows it: not only the best thing from where it stands, but the next several moves, which of seven corners, which of three creatures, how a fight should go, what to do on the way back. When something better appears it weighs finishing what it is already doing against switching, including what switching would cost; a slightly worse finish beats the illusion of a better first step, and a switch that is genuinely cheaper is taken. Two jobs that were always about even do not swap. A new thing entering the scene can become the next step when that is cheaper than finishing. Dropping is sometimes right: a creature it started on can be left if the player has gone so far that coming back afterwards would be the whole remaining job. A dummy that cannot hurt anyone is not a fight. While it waits for a line it likes, it is still doing something. Nothing about this is a rule for torches, or for fighting, or for coming home. A later action that depended on a hit, pickup or change to the world is reconsidered when that effect does not happen. Work the player has already done can satisfy the same purpose without making the companion repeat it. |
| **Choosing a place to work**, going where there is more to do, not to the nearest single job | Among several nearby places, it goes to the one where the most useful work sits together, a dark stretch with pots and something alive beats a single slime in an empty patch, without treating any one kind of job as the reason. Extra travel, the way back and whether the player is staying or leaving still decide; a rich place it cannot leave loses to a poorer place it can. Work on the way to a place, or on the way back from it, is leftover of whatever kind sits on that path, not a second expedition and not lighting's private courtesy. An interruption or the player moving on can change the answer before everything there is finished. |
| **Knowing what it cannot do**, the difference between "no" and "not sure yet" | It distinguishes an impossible action from one it has not worked out yet. Optional work, mining, chopping, collecting, pots, torches, starts only on a proven way to do it. Hunting may start moving once it knows a hit is possible from somewhere, without having pinned the standing tile, because the thing it is lining up will have moved; it still does not walk toward something no weapon could hit from anywhere. An unanswered search is not a mining or lighting walk, so it does something it can already do instead of walking at a maybe. Companionship and safety may still walk toward the player or a refuge while the whole route is unfinished. It does not promise a whole excursion merely because the first few steps are possible. A later world or ability change can make an earlier refusal worth reconsidering. |
| **Committing to a decision**, sticking with a choice unless something genuinely better appears | It looks decisive because continuing useful work has value, not because an old choice is compulsory. A fight is something it settles into and stays in while there is fighting worth doing, not something it flickers in and out of between shots. Remaining work, what switching would cost, danger and player relevance determine whether it continues or returns after interruption: finishing a slightly worse stretch beats the illusion of a better first step, and a stretch that is genuinely cheaper, including the cost of switching, is taken. One hit left can make finishing worthwhile regardless of who delivered the earlier hits; a vanished target, changed route or major threat can still make it leave. The nearer a job is to done, a creature almost dead, a spot almost reached, the more reason to finish it, so it does not turn around halfway because something else looked about as good, and two jobs that were always about even do not swap. A new thing entering the scene is not the same as those two swapping. Dropping is sometimes right: the player has gone so far that coming back after would be the whole remaining job. Sticking is not a rule that names a kind of work. Repeated small changes of the player's heading leave still-useful nearby work intact; when the player actually leaves, the companion can drop the remaining trip and join them without finishing an obsolete fight or vein. |
| **Firing position**, going somewhere it can shoot from | When it cannot hit from here, it moves so that a shot becomes possible. It starts that walk once it knows a hit could land from somewhere, without waiting to freeze the exact tile, because the thing it is lining up will have moved; it keeps going toward a useful region and can change its mind every step. Somewhere safe with no line is not a firing position. Where no place it can reach has a shot, it does not walk toward that creature at all. It does not go somewhere and then look for a shot: where it goes and the shot it means to take from there are one choice, and it keeps fighting from wherever it is on the way. Where it goes depends on the weapon it means to use: a weapon that flies flat and far fights from range, where it is safest, and a spread that only lands fully up close takes it in close; a piercing shot lines up along a row of enemies or a worm's body, a bouncing one drops low beside a group, and one that can bank off a wall is fired from where the bank reaches a creature it cannot see. |
| **Self-preservation**, staying alive | It is very hard to kill. It outranges nearly everything before hardmode, most ground creatures cannot reach it in the air at all, and it can usually put distance between itself and whatever is hitting it, so dying should take something that flies, shoots, or arrives in numbers. The world is not what is dangerous to it: no liquid hurts it, so water, honey, lava and shimmer are air. Where breaking off is genuinely not available, boxed in with something biting it, backing away is not the answer and it kills the thing instead. Getting hit costs more the less life it has, so it is visibly more careful at low health without any threshold being crossed. Safety wins ties against damage, always. |
| **Lighting the area**, keeping the place lit | It keeps the cave lit as a standing responsibility, travelling to unlit regions for that purpose and entering side passages to light them when it has established it can get back out. It works a dark place through rather than visiting it: one dark patch, then the next one on from it, and it comes back to the player only when nothing worth lighting is left in reach. When the player is the dark part it lights around the player first; when the player is carrying light it goes further afield. It never needs the player to place a torch first. It holds a torch only where its surroundings are dark, and puts it away the moment they are bright, whatever the light is. Lit means lit after everyone has left: a stretch bright only because of a torch in someone's hand, or a light pet, or its own held torch, is still unlit to it, while a placed torch, lava and daylight count. Held light may yield to a shot and resume; placed torches consume available supplies, so a lack of supplies does not justify an impossible placement. Anywhere in a dark place that the player's own smart cursor would offer a torch, it can place one and does, coming down to where a torch can actually go; a dark place it leaves unlit is one it has a reason to leave, never one it failed to notice. When it does not place one, the record says why, for that exact tile. |
| **Deciding what counts as a threat**, what it treats as dangerous | It distinguishes being visible from being able to cause harm. A creature known unable to reach either actor or affect them through its attacks is not an immediate threat; a wall alone does not establish that for an unfamiliar creature. How the creature moves, how soon it can actually arrive, how hard a hit it would land and how long it would take to put down all affect caution: a slime eight hops away is not the same as a bat at that distance with a clear line, and a slow thing that would take an age to kill is a different problem from either. When two things are both coming and there is not time to stop both, caution is which hit it would rather take, not which creature is the bigger project. Backing away from something that cannot connect is not caution. Danger to the player can matter even when the companion cannot currently shoot the source. |
| **Moving through the world**, actually getting to the place it chose, as opposed to choosing it | It arrives. It gathers speed over a moment rather than snapping to full pace, leans and curves through the bends, and eases off so it settles where it meant to rather than overshooting and swinging back; once there it keeps drifting gently around the spot rather than stopping dead, because it is never a thing parked in the air. At full pace it is exactly as fast as it ever was. Touching a wall is a brush and a slide, never a stop, and it never gets stuck: if the way it was taking closes, it takes another, and if there is none it says so. It moves like a thrown thing with a mind, not like a cursor: you can watch it drift in real time, and it never snaps between places. |
| **Protecting the player**, dealing with what is coming for the player | When something is coming for the player it goes and deals with it rather than standing nearby waiting, leashed so it does not chase across the map. It judges whether the player needs help at all, one zombie is the player's problem, three is not, and it can finish what it is doing first if the threat is not yet real. Protecting the player is not a separate way of fighting: it is the same fight with what is on the player counted highest, so there is one way it fights whether the danger is on the player, on itself or simply nearby. |
| **Breaking containers**, pots and the things in them | It can break a pot on the way past or make a worthwhile local trip for its possible contents, even in a well-lit area. The contents are uncertain until they appear. Access, danger, cargo space and the prospect of collecting the drops decide whether the trip is worthwhile; a pot does not require a separate expedition when it offers little. |
| **Dodging and kiting**, not taking damage while fighting | While fighting it avoids being hit rather than trading: moving away, rising above, dropping below, breaking line of sight behind terrain, and leaving a charge's line up or down rather than backing along it. It keeps shooting while it retreats. Getting out of the way rides on top of what it is doing: a dodge while drilling, fighting or following curves off the line and carries on with the job, and never means dropping the job to hide. It considers where an evasive move leaves it: avoiding one enemy must not put it in reach of another. Helping the player includes weighing the danger of the approach, so it can shoot from where it is, go over an obstruction or take another path rather than fly through a hostile. Safety matters during work and travel too, with minor damage still weighed against useful help. A harmless creature far away does not make it flinch, and getting out of harm's way never ends with it pressed against a wall going nowhere: a move that keeps it dry or unhit only by pinning it in place is not a way out. |
| **Opportunistic mining**, going and getting ore | When there is time, it seeks nearby ore it can reach through free air, hovers beside it and drills it while doing so remains worthwhile. It does not dig to ore behind solid rock; ore behind water or lava is ore it flies to. It can share the player's vein, reach any height the air allows, and notice a newly opened approach. Danger, player travel or changed feasibility can interrupt the work. It does not shoot while it mines, on the approach or between cuts; something worth fighting ends the trip, and the vein is still there afterwards. What it can break, and how fast, is what the pickaxe in its slot can break. |
| **Reporting what it is doing**, the companion's own account of itself | A glance tells the player that it is busy, from how it moves and what it gives off, a thruster trail while it travels, a beam on the tile it drills, streaks where it dodges, and never from words; the player does not need to read which job it chose. A companion that looks idle is something to fix in how it behaves, not something to label. |
| **Finding a route**, working out how to get somewhere, as opposed to performing the moves once it knows | It works out how to reach where it has decided to go through the air that is actually free, including routes whose first leg goes the wrong way: up and over a ceiling, further from the player in order to reach the player. It fits wherever there is a two-tile passage and nowhere there is a one-tile crack, and it prefers the middle of a passage to its walls. Where no route exists it establishes that rather than guessing, and where the world changes, a wall broken, a pool drained, it notices the new route within a second without being told. |
| **Staying with the player**, keeping pace without being underfoot | It keeps pace, picks its own line through the air, and is at the player's side rather than trailing. A fight does not wait on this: an enemy behind a player who is walking away is fought where it is, and the companion catches up once the fight is done. Being level with the player or ahead on their side and a little above is the ordinary case; it falls behind only when it stopped to do something. It is much faster than the player and gets faster as they do, so a new pair of boots or wings never leaves it behind. What it does not do is trail as a policy, orbit the player because it can, or hang in the player's tile as if that were the only legal place. The space around the player is a comfortable region that goes with them, roomy enough that it is not always on top of them, opening a little further the further it leans ahead, sitting mostly above them so it idles overhead even underground, leaning the way they are heading and always still containing them: inside it there is no pull, whatever the companion is doing, and outside it the pull grows with distance without ever on its own outweighing a job genuinely worth doing, until at the distance where it would lose them the only thing left is coming back the fast way. Keeping the player company is what it does only when there is nothing else to do: it never competes with work of any kind, so a slime ten tiles above them is fought, and so are zombies seven seconds away once nothing nearer is left. Among the jobs there are, the ones near the player and along where they are heading come first, zombies below a player going down before the ones above, so being near them still decides which job, never whether to work. Inside the region it is never parked: it moves through the whole of it carrying the player's pace, easing ahead and falling back, rising and dipping, busy or not. It never rises far above the player, because a fist-sized thing thirty tiles up is a thing the player cannot see. Nor does it skim the ground or hug the rock: wherever it settles or passes it keeps open air between itself and the nearest blocks and the nearest enemies, so it reads as a thing hovering in the room rather than resting on its floor, and it comes close to a block only when the job is at that block. In calm surroundings it can range further for useful work; when either of them may need help soon it stays within easier reach, even while gathering. Trapped harmless enemies need not keep it close. When there is no worthwhile job it drifts, dips and hovers around the player without seeking unexplored places for their own sake. When the player heads off, down a shaft, across a lake, it finishes what it is doing rather than dropping it to be near them, prefers the work along where they are going for its next job, and comes after them the fast way only once they are far enough that it would otherwise lose them. |
| **Looting**, picking things up | It gives ordinary items a chance to be collected without assuming it knows their importance to a modded playthrough. A nearby dirt block can justify two safe steps, while even ore can remain below an inescapable ledge. Collection is a fresh choice about time, risk, capacity and other useful work; it is not owed to every block it breaks. A small safe pickup may fit between tasks, while a serious fight or expensive detour takes precedence. Cargo remains accessible, and a full bag stops unnecessary excursions without silently discarding items. |
| **Weapon selection**, which weapon for which situation | It chooses between the two weapons the player has put in its slots by what they do to the targets in front of it: useful damage, finishing, several bodies in one line, threat removed. Weapon and victim are the same choice, because a piercing shot through three things can beat a harder hit on one. Any weapon handed to it joins that judgement through its actual properties rather than a special label, and it learns how each one flies and how hard each one shoves each kind of creature by using it, so the first shots with a new weapon may go wide or push something the wrong way and later ones do not. Where it stands and what it fires are judged together: a place is worth what the best shot any of its weapons could take from there is worth, so it moves to where its stronger weapon is safe to use rather than settling for the weaker one. Its two weapons are a pair, and what one leaves behind, a burn, a slow, a debuff the other hurts more, is learned from its own hits and used in sequence. How to use a weapon is learned from what its shots achieve, not only from how they fly: that a curving shot still rewards good aim, that a shot passing through one creature should be lined up on the next, that shards bursting behind a target should be sent into the creatures beyond it, how wide a spread of several projectiles really lands. Which weapon, which target, where to be and how to aim are one choice, made for creatures put down sooner, more of them hit and none pushed into the player, however unusual the weapon. Magic tires it: casting without pause weakens each cast down to about half and never stops it, so a tired staff loses to a fresh bow on its own. Ammo never runs out. A sword means closing with the target and pulling away after the hit. Weapons whose use is not simply aiming and releasing are refused by the slot rather than used badly. Going toward a creature does not pick the weapon either. It uses its weapons in an order when the order is worth more than either alone, and that order is a stretch it follows rather than a fresh pick every shot: a grenade dropped from above a group and, as it goes off, a piercing shot from low at the group's side through whatever is left; the sniper on a boss while its servants live and the shotgun on the servants, then the shotgun on the boss up close once they are gone; a bow that shoves a slime off the ore, then shurikens because those hurt more once it is off. While it waits for a line it likes it still uses a weapon, or it does leftover at its feet; hanging empty-handed until the perfect moment is not an order. It shoots only while fighting and it fights readily; drilling is finished or left before a fight starts, and it takes its torch back out when the fight is over. |
| **Recovering when it cannot follow**, what it does when the player is unreachable | When the player goes where it cannot, through a gap too narrow for it, it remains useful among nearby trees, enemies, drops, darkness and ore rather than pressing at the gap. Local work still competes with danger, remaining effort and the importance of rejoining the player. It can leave unfinished work when reunion matters more. Its last-resort return is continuous and never teleports. When no worthwhile action remains it waits as close to the player as the terrain allows, and says so. |
| **Changing the world**, what it is allowed to alter, and where it stops | It may break trees, ore and pots, place torches and open doors. That list is closed and nothing joins it, no digging to reach something, no bridging a gap, no tunnelling, no opening chests, no planting. The reason is not caution about any one of those: the moment the set is open the companion is remodelling a world the player did not ask it to touch, and there is no version of that a player can predict. Within the permitted set it still stops at the player's home, nothing is broken or placed inside somewhere the player lives, so the lighting it does in a cave it does not do in a bedroom. Where a job needs an edit outside the set, the job is simply not available, which is the same answer as any other thing it cannot reach. |
| **Speed**, using everything its body can currently do, and knowing what it cannot | It is much faster than the player and its speed follows theirs, so boots and wings on the player make it faster in step and it is never left behind. It never climbs far above the player. No liquid hurts or slows it: a flooded shaft is a shaft and a lava lake is a room, and always has been. It never declines something it can do. Player-derived changes to reach, tool power and defensive strength affect what it attempts just as consistently as its own mastery unlocks. |
| **Doors**, knowing a door is not a wall | A door it can open is not an obstacle and never appears in its thinking as one, so a route through a closed door is an ordinary route and it opens the door and goes through the way a person does. It is specific about which doors those are: one with something hard against both sides cannot open and genuinely is a wall, while one with room on the far side opens and is not. Getting that wrong in either direction is visible, refusing a route through a door that would have opened, or pressing at one that will not. |
| **Getting up after being downed**, how long a down costs | Being downed takes the companion out for a short while and then stops costing anything. The player helping speeds it up and is never required. A glance at the companion while it is down shows that get-up is underway, whether the player is standing beside it or it is getting up on its own. |
| **Opportunistic chopping**, going and getting wood | It seeks nearby trees when there is time, approaches safely and weighs the remaining work against danger and the player's activity. It usually chooses a different tree from the one the player is cutting, though sharing an ore vein is fine. Active axe work keeps the hand occupied coherently. Felling and collecting are separately reconsidered when circumstances change; neither a fixed tree count nor past effort forces the rest of the job. |
| **Growing stronger**, what levelling changes, and what it never changes | As it levels it gets tougher and faster. Its offence grows the way the player's does: by the player handing it a better weapon, pickaxe or axe. Some of what it earns protects the player directly: a share of a hit the player would have taken lands on it instead, both of them take less the closer it stays, and some of what it deals comes back as healing. What never changes with level is its judgement. The companion at its first level and the companion at its last choose the same things for the same reasons, what widens is what it is able to do and survive, never what it is willing to do, so nothing about how it behaves is ever locked behind a purchase, and no upgrade makes it guard better, follow closer or decide differently. Where an upgrade meets a habit, the habit gives way on its own: a faster body takes the longer trip it used to pass up. It never gets strong enough to fight a boss for the player, and it is never so weak that it stops mattering. |
| **Using handed gear**, fighting and working with what the player puts in its slots | It has no weapon or tool of its own. Two weapons, a pickaxe and an axe go in its slots, real items from the game or from any mod, and it uses them: any weapon that is aimed and released, it fires or swings; the pickaxe and axe decide what it can drill and how fast, exactly as they would for the player. It refuses, visibly, what it cannot use rather than using it badly. It learns how a new weapon flies by firing it. Ammo never runs out. Nothing else is ever equipped on it. A player who has not yet found where its gear goes can find the slots without being told. |
| **Tiring**, what sustained magic costs it | Its mana is the player's mana. Casting spends it and it comes back on its own; when it is low each cast lands weaker, down to about half at empty, and it never refuses to cast. A tired staff loses to a fresh bow in its own judgement, so it swaps rather than plinking at half strength. |

### Guarantees and limits: what it promises, and where it deliberately stops

- **Singleplayer only.** No netcode; the companion follows the local player.
- **It never teleports** in ordinary play; recovery flight past the leash distance is continuous and visible.
- **It works within 100 tiles of you and flies home past 125.** Neither distance is a setting.
- **What it may change about the world is a closed set**: break ore, trees and pots, place torches, and open doors. It never digs ordinary terrain, never opens chests, and never plants.
- **Four slots and no more**: two weapons, a pickaxe and an axe. No armour, no accessories, no ammo slot.
- **Bed-containing rooms are protected** from its edits; open or oversized structures get conservative protection near the bed. This is early protection logic, not a claim to recognise every player-built base.

## Getting started: build it as a tModLoader mod source

### Requirements: Terraria with tModLoader

Terraria and tModLoader on Steam, and the .NET SDK tModLoader builds with. The mod is singleplayer.

### Install: clone into tModLoader's ModSources folder and build

```bash
cd "<your tModLoader folder>/ModSources"
git clone https://github.com/Capataina/AICompanion.git
```

Then build it from tModLoader (Workshop, Develop Mods, Build) or, with the game closed, run `dotnet build` in the folder. Success is "Multi... Player?" appearing in the Mods list, enabled.

### Quick start: one chat command

Enter a singleplayer world and type `/companion` in chat. The orb appears near you and starts following. Open its profile card from the notch and hand it a weapon, a pickaxe and an axe on the Inventory page.

### Usage: settings, the brain inspector and local telemetry

- **Work preferences** live on the profile card: mining and chopping each Off, Mimic (only what you are doing) or Auto, and torch placement on or off. They are saved per character.
- **The brain inspector** is bound under Controls, Mod Controls (default `[`). It opens a menu of world drawings (enemy forecasts, projectile paths, route steps, aiming, movement alternatives, attention and decision scores) that keep drawing after the menu closes.
- **Local telemetry** is a mod setting. When it is on before you enter a world, the session is recorded under the tModLoader save folder's `ModSources/AICompanion/Telemetry`; nothing uploads. For a bug report, leave the world and send the files sharing that run's timestamp.

## How it works: observe, choose a course, move, act, sixty times a second

### Architecture: one loop, with one movement boundary

1. **It looks.** One account of the world: where you are and how you have been moving, nearby enemies and projectiles and whom they can reach, drops and whether there is room for them, how dark the open air is, and its own state.
2. **It checks whether it is about to be hit.** Avoiding a hit never takes the body away from its job; it bends whatever the body is doing.
3. **It decides what is worth doing as a plan, not a pick.** It searches short ordered sequences of concrete jobs (an ore tile, a tree, a drop, dark air, a shot) and prices each by what it would actually cause: the work done, the harm that would reach you and itself on the way, and the time it would take. It finishes the course it chose and decides again at the end.
4. **The bound step says what kind of place the body goes to**, and position selection turns that into a point in free space to hover at.
5. **Movement gets it there**, with one body for every purpose: a route through free space wide enough for the orb, steering that carries momentum, a slow hover on arrival, and one motor that is the only thing allowed to move the NPC.
6. **The hands act.** Weapons fire only while it is fighting; the pickaxe and axe swing during work; a torch fills an empty hand in the dark.
7. **It watches what actually happened** (the block took damage, the item reached the bag) and the next loop starts from that, not from what it hoped.

```
   world ──► observe (frozen for the life of one decision)
                 │
                 ▼
     discover work across ore · trees · drops · dark air · shots
                 ▼
     search short ordered courses, priced by projected consequences
                 ▼
     keep the course while its next step still holds
                 ▼
     bound step ──► a kind of place ──► choose a hover point
                 ▼
     plan and steer ──► one control grant (body + hand) ──► motor ──► NPC
                                   │
            observed results feed the next loop ◄──────────────┘
```

The stack is C# on tModLoader. The orb's contact with terrain is the mod's own circle-against-tiles test with the engine's box collision switched off, run identically in the game and in every headless tool, so there is one body and nothing to keep in agreement with it.

### Key concepts: the brain's vocabulary

- **Course**: a short ordered sequence of concrete jobs, priced by its consequences.
- **Sense**: a fact the whole brain reads (light, reach, your intent) instead of each part answering privately.
- **Intent region**: a box carried ahead of your feet in the direction you are travelling; "near you" means near where you are going.
- **Reach flood**: where the orb can fly to, flooded over free space, answering reachable, not yet known, or proven absent.
- **Route, usable position, productive effect**: three different facts. Finding a way is not arriving, and arriving is not being useful.

### Invariants: what is always true

- One motor is the only writer of the NPC's movement, and one control grant decides body and hand together.
- An unfinished search is "not yet known", never "absent"; optional work does not start on an unanswered question.
- Danger to you and danger to the companion are measured separately, and neither stands in for the other.
- Only a native effect the game observed counts as work done.

## Design: every surface says the minimum a player needs in the moment

The interface keeps the game's own look rather than a custom skin. The profile card is one panel whose body swaps between pages, never several panels opening one another; it drags by its title bar, the same gesture as the notch. Slot grids use fixed-size slots and let the column count follow the width. Leftover room is not filled: the answer to an empty-looking gap was a shorter card. A node's panel on the mastery wheel is its name, what it does, a Learn button and a level bar, and nothing that explains the surface to itself. The mastery wheel is laid out by hand, every lane the same shape rotated, because a force-directed layout produces a characterless spider diagram. Until the orb's own art exists, it is drawn with a deliberate placeholder so the body can be judged in play first.

## Development: one command checks everything, headless

```bash
just test       # the whole headless check: build, the movement-boundary check and every instrument; nothing launches the game
just verify     # the whole headless check, then the committed scenario corpus through the real planner
just case "<name>"    # run one named case on its own
just flake "<name>"   # measure how often an intermittent case fires
```

Success is `just test` exiting 0. Nothing in it launches the game or packages the mod; a fresh game launch picks up a `dotnet build` made with the game closed.
